## Introduction

The English name for Hematite, *Hematite*, is said to originate from the Greek word "**haima (blood)**." While its mirror-like polished surface appears deep silvery-black, when powdered, it turns a vibrant reddish-brown. This led ancient Greeks and Romans to call it the "**blood stone**" and carry it as a talisman to stop bleeding and secure victory in battle.

In ancient Egypt, it was used as a ritual tool by priests as a "**rest the head**" stone, and many hematite statues have been unearthed from tombs. During the European Middle Ages, it is said that knights sewed it inside their armor as a "**talisman to prevent bleeding on the battlefield**." Even today, it's a primary choice for themes of "**victory, vitality, and grounding**."

## Stone Properties and Meaning

Hematite is associated with the properties of "**Victory, Vitality, and Grounding**." It has long been cherished worldwide as the "Stone of Victory and Vitality," an ancient warrior's shield.

Roman soldiers are said to have powdered hematite and applied it to their bodies, casting a "**spell to prevent bleeding**." In medieval Italy, Physician Galen reportedly prescribed hematite as a "**medicinal stone for stopping bleeding**." Native Americans in North America are also said to have used the red pigment from hematite for their chiefs' ceremonial face paint.

As its Japanese name "赤鉄鉱" (sekitekko, meaning "red iron ore") suggests, its composition is literally **iron oxide (Fe₂O₃)** itself. Due to its weighty, metallic feel, reminiscent of the Earth's core, it's positioned in the modern spiritual market as a "**primary stone for balancing the First (Root) Chakra**."

## Characteristics by Origin

Hematite varies in texture and hardness depending on its origin.

-   **Brazilian**: Most commonly circulated. Features a consistent silvery-black mirror finish.
-   **Cumbrian, UK**: Famous for its spherical crystals known as "**Kidney Ore**" (or botryoidal hematite).
-   **Minnesotan, USA**: From the Mesabi Range. Known for strong luster, favored for accessories.
-   **Moroccan**: Pyrite-associated specimens are occasionally found.
-   **Magnetite-mixed specimens**: These types have magnetic properties. Be cautious, as "**Magnetic Hematite**" is often synthetically produced.

## How to Spot Fakes and Artificial Stones

-   **Hematite that sticks to a magnet is almost certainly artificial.** Natural hematite has very little magnetic property. Be cautious, as "Magnetic Hematite" is often a synthetically produced ferrite magnet.
-   If the surface is perfectly smooth and it feels light, it might be a copy made of glass or resin.
-   While turning reddish-brown (blood-red color) when powdered is a sign of true hematite, testing beads would be a destructive examination. Therefore, it's more practical to buy from a reputable store.
-   Purchasing with a certificate of authenticity from a trusted specialty store offers peace of mind. Please also refer to our [Guide to Spotting Fake and Artificial Stones](/blog/spot-fake-stones/).

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q. Does Hematite stick to a magnet?**
A. Natural hematite has **very little magnetic property**. Commercially available items that strongly stick to a magnet are usually "maghemite" or synthetic products.

**Q. What bead size is recommended for a bracelet?**
A. For enjoying the luster of its mirror finish, **6-10mm** is a classic choice. Since it's a weighty stone, your wrist might get tired if the beads are too large.

**Q. Is it suitable for both men and women?**
A. Yes, absolutely! Its sharp silvery-black color makes it popular with both men and women. Men often favor combinations with [Onyx](/blog/onyx/), while women often prefer combinations with [Garnet](/blog/garnet/).

**Q. Can it rust?**
A. Since it's iron oxide, if exposed to moisture or sweat continuously, rust may appear on its surface. We recommend wiping it with a dry cloth after sweating.
