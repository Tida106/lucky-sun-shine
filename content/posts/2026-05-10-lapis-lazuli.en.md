## Introduction

Lapis Lazuli is one of the **world's oldest** crystals. Traces of its use as an adornment have been found in Mesopotamian ruins dating back to around 7000 BCE. In ancient Egypt, it was highly prized as the "**stone of the heavens**," and Lapis Lazuli was intricately inlaid around the eyes and collar of Tutankhamun's golden mask.

Even in the world of painting, the blue in Vermeer's Renaissance masterpiece, **"Girl with a Turban,"** comes from 'ultramarine' pigment, made by crushing Lapis Lazuli. At the time, it was more expensive than gold, making it an indispensable stone in cultural history.

Strictly speaking, it's not a single mineral but a unique **rock** composed of multiple minerals. The golden sparkles are iron sulfide (pyrite), and the white spots are calcite. Afghan Lapis Lazuli is considered the highest quality, and the Sar-e-Sang mine is one of the world's oldest mines, where humanity has been extracting for **over 6,000 years**.

## <span id="meaning"></span>Stone Lore, History, and Cultural Background

Its lore includes "**Truth, Wisdom, and Good Fortune**."

In ancient Egypt, it was considered the "**symbol of the celestial realm**" and used as an amulet for royalty and priests. Lapis Lazuli was also used in scarabs, which were placed in tombs as protective charms. It appears in Homer's epics and was traded in Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley Civilization, and ancient China.

In Europe, it enriched the history of painting as the raw material for **"Ultramarine."** It was used to paint the blue robes of the Virgin Mary in medieval Christian art and was such an expensive material that the cost of Lapis Lazuli pigment was explicitly stated in church commissions.

In Japan, it arrived with the spread of Buddhism under the name "**Ruri**" and became a symbol of the 'Eastern Pure Land of Lapis Lazuli Light,' the paradise of Bhaisajyaguru (Medicine Buddha). It is counted as one of the Seven Treasures of Buddhism.

## <span id="care"></span>Cleansing Methods and Care

Recommended methods:

-   **Clear quartz chips** — The safest and suitable for all stones.
-   **White sage smoke** — 1 to 2 minutes is sufficient.
-   **Moonlight bath** — Place near a window three days before and after the full moon.

Methods to avoid:

-   **Soaking in water or running water** — Lazurite and calcite are vulnerable to water; prolonged exposure can lead to deterioration.
-   **Directly burying in coarse salt** — Risk of surface discoloration due to chemical reactions.
-   **Prolonged direct sunlight** — Can cause fading and cracking.

For daily care, simply wipe gently with a soft cloth. It's a golden rule to wipe away sweat and sebum with a **dry cloth**.

Key points for choosing:

-   **Color** — A deep cobalt blue, known as "**royal blue**," is considered the highest quality.
-   **Pyrite** — Lapis Lazuli with a moderate scattering of golden pyrite is beautiful and highly valued.
-   **Calcite (white spots)** — Too much can lower its value.
-   **Origin** — Afghan Lapis Lazuli is the highest quality. Chilean Lapis Lazuli often has more calcite and is more affordably priced.

## <span id="fake"></span>Identifying Fakes and Dyed Stones

-   **Dyeing treatment** — Darkly colored Lapis-like stones are likely dyed. Almost all of these are dyed howlite.
-   **Acetone wipe test** — If a dyed stone is wiped with an acetone-soaked cotton swab, the color will rub off.
-   If there are no pyrite sparkles, it might be a different mineral like **sodalite**.
-   Purchasing with a gemological certificate from a reputable specialty store offers peace of mind.

## <span id="faq"></span>Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. What's the difference between Lapis Lazuli and blue sodalite?**

A. While similar in appearance, sodalite **does not contain pyrite** and is a softer blue stone. Lapis Lazuli is characterized by "blue ground with golden specks," while sodalite has "blue ground with white streaks." Lapis Lazuli is generally more expensive.

**Q2. Is it okay to wear Lapis Lazuli during an unlucky year (Yakudoshi)?**

A. On the contrary, it is **highly recommended**. Since ancient times, it has been a stone symbolizing "warding off evil" and "strength to overcome life's turning points," so there's a global tradition of wearing it during unlucky years or times of transition.

**Q3. Is extremely cheap Lapis Lazuli suspicious?**

A. If a bracelet is priced under ¥2,000 (around $15-20 USD), it's highly likely to be **dyed howlite**. Genuine Lapis Lazuli rough itself is expensive, so be wary of extremely low prices.

**Q4. Can I still get ultramarine pigment made from Lapis Lazuli today?**

A. Yes, it's sold as "natural ultramarine" at art supply stores. It's an expensive pigment, costing thousands to tens of thousands of yen per gram, but it offers a deeper, more mystical color than synthetic ultramarine and is cherished by contemporary artists.

**Q5. How should I use Lapis Lazuli before an important decision?**

A. As you mentally articulate the question you need to decide on, hold the Lapis Lazuli in your left palm for a few tens of seconds. Traditionally, this method doesn't immediately give you an answer, but rather makes it **easier for you to realize which direction your true self is leaning towards**.
