## Introduction

Meiji Jingu Shrine, located right next to Harajuku in Tokyo, is the capital's largest power spot, boasting **approximately 700,000 square meters of sacred forest**. The **about 100,000 trees** planted at its creation in 1920 (Taisho 9) have, despite being an artificial forest, formed a deep, primeval-like green over 100 years. It ranks among **Japan's top shrines for New Year's visitors (over 3 million annually)** and is a symbol of Tokyo, visited by musicians and heads of state from around the world. This article brings together its **history, key visiting points, FAQs, and an editor's note** all on one page.

## <span id="history"></span>History and Origins

After the demise of Emperor Meiji and Empress Shoken, Meiji Jingu Shrine was **founded on November 1, 1920 (Taisho 9)** to enshrine their divine spirits. The sacred forest was conceived as an **"eternal forest"** through a grand afforestation plan designed by forest scientists such as Seiroku Honda and Takanori Hongo. **Approximately 100,000 trees** were donated from across the nation and planted using methods that replicate natural forests, which were highly regarded. Although originally estimated to take 150 years to complete, the current forest is already considered to be in a **"self-regenerating primeval-like state"**.

The shrine buildings were destroyed during air raids in World War II but were rebuilt in 1958 with donations from the public after the war. It also served as an important stage for the **Reiwa enthronement ceremonies**.

## <span id="highlight"></span>Highlights and Must-Sees

- **Kiyomasa's Well (Kiyomasa-no-Ido)** — a famous power spot in Tokyo, said to have been dug by Kato Kiyomasa
- **Treasure Museum** — featuring Emperor Meiji's imperial carriage and other artifacts
- **Meiji Jingu Gyoen Garden** — including Kakun-tei Teahouse, South Pond, and an Iris Garden
- **Martial Arts Dojo (Shiseikan)**

## <span id="point"></span>Key Visiting Points

- The center of the path is for the deities, so **walk along the sides**.
- **Visit Kiyomasa's Well early in the morning** when the air is clear, and photos turn out beautifully.
- At the Meoto-Kusu (Married Couple Camphor Trees), it's believed that **couples or partners praying side-by-side** will be blessed with good relationships.
- For Hatsumode (New Year's visit), **early morning to late morning on New Year's Day** sees the peak crowds, and the first three days are crowded all day.
- Exorcism (Oharai) and prayers to ward off evil (Yakuyoke Kito) are accepted at the shrine office at any time.

## <span id="manner"></span>Basic Etiquette for Shrine Visits

1.  Bow once before passing through the torii gate or main gate.
2.  The center of the path is for the deities, so walk along the sides.
3.  At the Chozuya (purification fountain), purify your left hand, then your right hand, rinse your mouth, then your left hand again, and finally rinse the ladle.
4.  **Two bows, two claps, one bow.**
5.  Keep personal wishes modest, focusing primarily on gratitude.

## <span id="faq"></span>Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. Is Kiyomasa's Well truly famous as a power spot?**

A. It's a spring believed to have been dug by Kato Kiyomasa, and one of Tokyo's most pristine waters. A rumor once spread that **setting it as your phone's wallpaper would improve your luck**. An admission fee of 500 yen for the Meiji Jingu Gyoen Garden is required.

**Q2. When is Hatsumode (New Year's visit) less crowded?**

A. It's relatively less crowded on New Year's Day between **3 PM and 4 PM**, or **from January 4th onwards**. Waiting 3 to 4 hours is not uncommon from midnight on New Year's Eve until morning.

**Q3. Where are the Meoto-Kusu (Married Couple Camphor Trees) located?**

A. The Meoto-Kusu, or sacred Married Couple Camphor Trees, are the **two camphor trees** located to the left, just before the main shrine building. Their appearance, bound together by a shimenawa rope, symbolizes matchmaking and marital harmony.

**Q4. Are weddings and Shichi-Go-San ceremonies possible here?**

A. Yes, various Shinto rituals are performed, including **Shinto-style weddings, Omiyamairi (baby's first shrine visit), Shichi-Go-San (children's festival), Jichinsai (groundbreaking ceremonies), and Yakuyoke Kito (prayers to ward off evil)**. Reservations are required in advance (please inquire at the shrine office).

**Q5. Is there parking nearby?**

A. There is a paid parking lot at the North Approach entrance. However, the surrounding roads get **extremely crowded** on weekends and and public holidays, so using public transportation is highly recommended.
