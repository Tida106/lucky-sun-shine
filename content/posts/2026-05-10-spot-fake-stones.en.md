## Introduction

The crystal market has a significant amount of **glass fakes, dyed products, artificial stones, and treated stones** in circulation. The risk is especially higher for more expensive stones, making it difficult for an amateur to tell the difference. This article will explain **8 checkpoints even beginners can use** and places you should avoid buying from.

## 4 Categories You Should Know First

### 1. Natural Stones (Authentic)

Stones formed naturally deep within the earth. It's common for them to have **color variations, inclusions, and tiny cracks**.

### 2. Heat and Radiation-Treated Stones (Legal and Common)

These are natural stones that have undergone heat or radiation treatment to enhance their color. The majority of the [**Citrine**](/blog/citrine/) market falls into this category (it's actually heat-treated [Amethyst](/blog/amethyst/)). There's no problem as long as it's clearly stated as "treated."

### 3. Dyed Stones (Caution Needed)

Natural stones colored with dye. Common with [**Lapis Lazuli**](/blog/lapis-lazuli/), [**Turquoise**](/blog/turquoise/), and Howlite. You should suspect dyeing for inexpensive items without a gemological certificate.

### 4. Artificial Stones and Glass Fakes (No Go)

These are complete fakes made from glass or synthetic resin. Their characteristic is that the color and patterns are **too uniform**.

## 8 Checkpoints Anyone Can Use

### Check 1: Color Uniformity

-   **Natural Stones**: Have color variations, gradients, and impurities.
-   **Fakes**: The color is **too uniform**, and the hue is excessively vibrant.

Example: Authentic [Lapis Lazuli](/blog/lapis-lazuli/) has golden [Pyrite](/blog/pyrite/) scattered within its blue. **A completely solid blue** is highly likely to be dyed or fake.

### Check 2: Inclusions and Cracks

-   **Natural Stones**: You can see small crystals, cracks, or air bubbles inside.
-   **Fakes**: The interior is **completely clear**, or air bubbles are arranged in a uniform pattern (characteristic of glass).

Stones with **inclusions** like the golden needles in [Rutilated Quartz](/blog/rutilated-quartz/) or the color bands in [Fluorite](/blog/fluorite/) are often **easier to distinguish from fakes**.

### Check 3: Temperature

Natural stones feel **cool to the touch**. If it warms up quickly after being held in your palm for 5 seconds, it might be plastic or glass.

### Check 4: Hardness (Mohs Hardness)

If you can lightly scratch it with your fingernail or a coin, it might not be the stone it claims to be.
-   [Clear Quartz](/blog/clear-quartz/) (Mohs 7): Cannot be scratched by a steel file.
-   [Rose Quartz](/blog/rose-quartz-meaning/) (Mohs 7): Same as above.
-   [Malachite](/blog/malachite/) (Mohs 3.5-4): Can be scratched by a copper coin.

*Note: We don't recommend this check after purchase due to the risk of damaging your precious stone.*

### Check 5: Weight

Natural stones have **solid density**, so they feel heavier than fakes made of glass or resin. It's easier to tell if you compare it with an authentic stone of the same size.

### Check 6: Price

-   **Obviously too cheap**: High probability of being fake or dyed.
-   Example: An authentic [Lapis Lazuli](/blog/lapis-lazuli/) bracelet usually costs ¥3,000 to ¥10,000, but it's being sold for **¥500**.

### Check 7: Store Reliability

-   Mineral specialty stores and crystal specialty stores are generally reliable.
-   Be wary of roadside stalls and souvenir shops in tourist areas.
-   Personal listings on flea market apps are not recommended unless accompanied by a gemological certificate.

### Check 8: Presence of a Gemological Certificate

For expensive stones (over ¥10,000), a **gemological certificate** is desirable. A certificate should state:
-   Mineral name (the mineralogically accurate name)
-   Presence and type of treatment
-   Origin (may not always be stated)

## Ranking of Stones with Many Fakes and Treated Items

### Stones with a Particularly High Number of Fakes

1.  [**Lapis Lazuli**](/blog/lapis-lazuli/): Dyed (fake dyed Howlite)
2.  [**Turquoise**](/blog/turquoise/): Reconstituted and Dyed (powder pressed with resin)
3.  [**Malachite**](/blog/malachite/): A certain number of dyed products are in circulation.
4.  [**Amethyst**](/blog/amethyst/): Those with overly deep color might be synthetic stones.
5.  [**Citrine**](/blog/citrine/): The majority of the market is heat-treated Amethyst (treated item).

### Treatments are Common, but "Selling as Treated" is the Rule

-   [Blue Topaz](/blog/blue-topaz/): Radiation treatment of colorless topaz is common.
-   [Aquamarine](/blog/aquamarine/): Heat treatment to enhance color.
-   [Carnelian](/blog/carnelian/): Heat treatment is common.

These are **legal and common** as long as they are clearly stated as "natural and treated."

## Places to Avoid Buying From

-   **Souvenir shops in tourist areas**: Price and actual quality often diverge.
-   **Roadside stalls**: No guarantee.
-   **"Special price" online stores**: Be wary of items drastically cheaper than the regular price.
-   **Personal listings on flea market apps** (without a gemological certificate).

## Reliable Places to Buy From

-   **Mineral specialty stores**: Such as Ikebukuro Show Stone, Shinjuku Tokyu Hands, etc.
-   **Department store jewelry sections**: More expensive, but reliable.
-   **Crystal specialty stores** (with physical locations).
-   **Mineral shows**: Held several times a year, gathering specialized dealers.
-   **Large online retailers**: Choose stores with good reviews and a proven track record.

## Editorial Team's View: "Price" is the Most Honest Signal

We've listed 8 checkpoints, but to be honest with you from the editorial team — **the only reliable way for a beginner to spot fakes is by looking at the "price."**

Judging color uniformity, inclusions, and temperature isn't accurate unless you're **used to handling genuine stones for comparison**. On the other hand, anyone can look up market prices, so by simply following **the rule "don't buy anything drastically cheaper than the market price,"** you can avoid almost all fakes and dyed products.

### Price Guidelines for 5 Stones with a High Number of Fakes and Dyed Items

| Stone | Bracelet 8mm Market Price | Suspicious Price Range |
| :-------------- | :------------------------ | :----------------------------------------- |
| **Lapis Lazuli** | ¥3,000 – ¥10,000 | Below ¥1,500 → Suspected dyed Howlite |
| **Turquoise** | ¥4,000 – ¥15,000 | Below ¥2,000 → Suspected reconstituted and dyed |
| **Malachite** | ¥3,000 – ¥8,000 | Below ¥1,500 → Suspected dyed |
| **Jade (Genuine Jadeite)** | ¥10,000 – Hundreds of thousands | Below ¥3,000 → Suspected dyed or substitute stone |
| **Morion** | ¥3,000 – ¥8,000 | Below ¥1,500 → Suspected synthetic stone sold as black quartz |

Products like a "**¥500 Lapis bracelet**" have a **near 100% probability** of being dyed. And dyed products, frankly, have almost zero cultural or symbolic value — that's the editorial team's honest assessment.

### Editorial Team's Recommended Purchase Checklist

Before buying, check if you can answer these 5 questions:

1.  Do you know the **store's location and contact information**? (Be wary of online shops without a physical address.)
2.  Is the **origin** stated?
3.  Is the **presence or absence of treatment** (heat, dye, coating) clearly specified?
4.  Is it drastically cheaper compared to the **market price of the same stone at other stores**?
5.  Is the **return policy** clearly stated?

If all 5 questions are "Yes," you should be fine to buy. If even one is "No," the editorial team recommends looking at other stores a bit more.

### Common Misconceptions

-   ❌ "**If the store staff says it's genuine, then it's genuine.**" → ✅ The staff themselves might be mistaken. A **gemological certificate** provides objective assurance.
-   ❌ "**Heat-treated items = fakes.**" → ✅ Heat treatment is a **standard process recognized by the industry**. It's legitimate if clearly stated as "treated."
-   ❌ "**If it comes with a gemological certificate, it's definitely genuine.**" → ✅ Only if it's from a reputable gemological institution. **"Certificates" created by individuals** are unreliable.
-   ❌ "**Mineral shows = only genuine items.**" → ✅ Major dealers are trustworthy, but **individual booths** may also have dyed products. Don't let your guard down even at shows.

## Summary

-   Crystals fall into 4 categories: **natural, treated, dyed, and fake**.
-   Checks anyone can do include **color uniformity, inclusions, temperature, and price**.
-   For expensive stones, a **gemological certificate** provides peace of mind.
-   Be wary of tourist spots, roadside vendors, and individual listings on flea markets; specialty stores and mineral shows are recommended.

"**Don't buy stones that are too cheap**" — this is the best tip for avoiding fakes. Even if it costs a bit more, choosing **one reliable stone** will give you a partner you can cherish for a long time.
