## Introduction

Togakushi Shrine is one of Shinshu's most venerable ancient shrines, with five distinct sanctuaries—**Okusha, Chusha, Hokosha, Kuzuryu-sha, and Hinomikosha**—scattered at the foot of the sacred **Mount Togakushi** (1904m elevation). Its origins are deeply rooted in the **"Ama-no-Iwato"** myth, with **Ame-no-Tajikarao-no-Mikoto**, the deity who famously hurled away the rock door, enshrined as the main deity of Okusha. The **over 400-year-old cedar avenue** leading to Okusha Shrine is a majestic path, designated among the Forestry Agency's "100 Giant Trees of Forests" and "100 New Japanese Street Trees," and its mystical atmosphere leaves many visitors awestruck. This article compiles everything you need to know, including the **history, features of the five shrines, FAQs, and an editorial note**, all on one convenient page.

## <span id="history"></span>History and Origins

According to Togakushi Shrine's traditions, when Amaterasu Omikami (the Sun Goddess) hid herself in the Ama-no-Iwato (Heavenly Rock Cave), **Ame-no-Tajikarao-no-Mikoto** forcibly opened the rock door and threw it away. It is said that this rock door landed on what is now **Mount Togakushi**. This event is the origin of both the shrine's name and its connection to this profound myth.

From the Heian period onwards, **Mount Togakushi** flourished as a sacred site for **Shugendo** (a syncretic religion of mountain asceticism), rivaling the influence of **Mount Hiei and Mount Koya**. It is said that at its peak, the mountain housed thousands of warrior monks. Following the Meiji government's Shinbutsu-Bunri (separation of Shinto and Buddhism) decree, the site was separated from its Buddhist temples and reorganized into the current five-shrine system. Togakushi Shrine has a unique history, where mountain worship, Shinto, and Shugendo are intricately interwoven.

## <span id="sugi"></span>Cedar Avenue and Zuijinmon Gate

The most famous feature of Togakushi Shrine is the **Cedar Avenue leading to Okusha Shrine**. This awe-inspiring path stretches for approximately **500 meters, lined with cedars over 400 years old**, earning its place in the Forestry Agency's "100 Giant Trees of Forests" and "100 New Japanese Street Trees." The **Zuijinmon Gate (a vermilion-lacquered gate with a thatched roof)**, located midway, is a popular photo spot, and it's especially enchanting enveloped in a mysterious mist on a foggy morning.

The Okusha Shrine approach is approximately **2 km one way**, taking about 40 minutes on foot from the entrance to the shrine. The latter half involves a steep climb up stone steps, so comfortable shoes and clothing are highly recommended.

## <span id="point"></span>Pilgrimage Tips

- If possible, try to visit all five shrines on the **Goshameguri (Five Shrines Pilgrimage)** (the full route takes half a day to a full day on foot).
- The cedar avenue to Okusha Shrine is most majestic in the **early morning**. It's even more mystical on a foggy morning.
- Don't miss the **Three Cedars** within Chusha Shrine's precincts and, of course, **Togakushi soba**.
- Goshuin (shrine stamps) are available at each of the five shrines, and dedicated Goshuincho (stamp books) are also available.

## <span id="access"></span>Access

By Train: From JR and Hokuriku Shinkansen "**Nagano Station**," take an Alpico Kotsu bus (Togakushi Line) for about 1 hour.

By Car: Approximately 20 minutes from Shinanomachi IC on the Joshinetsu Expressway, or about 1 hour from Nagano IC.

**Parking**: Parking lots are available at Okusha, Chusha, and Hokosha Shrines (some are paid).

## <span id="season"></span>Best Season and Time to Visit

**May to October** is the best season. The fresh greenery in May and the autumn leaves in October are especially beautiful. During **winter (December to April)**, the Okusha Shrine approach may be closed due to heavy snow, and a visit might require snowshoes or specialized gear. The cedar avenue in the **early morning** is quiet and majestic, making it a favorite for photographers.

## <span id="manner"></span>Basic Etiquette for Shrine Visits

1. Bow once before passing through the torii gate.
2. The center of the path is for the deities; walk along the sides.
3. At the temizuya (water purification basin), cleanse your left hand, then your right hand, rinse your mouth, cleanse your left hand again, and then cleanse the dipper.
4. Perform **two bows, two claps, and one bow** when praying.
5. Refrain from loud talking on the cedar avenue and be considerate of others when taking photos.
6. On mountain paths, take your trash with you and stay on designated trails.

## <span id="faq"></span>Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. Can I visit all five shrines in one day?**

A. Yes, it's possible if you have a car, but since the round trip to Okusha Shrine takes about 2 hours, we recommend starting around **8:00 AM**. If you're doing the entire pilgrimage on foot, it will take half a day to a full day.

**Q2. Is the Okusha Shrine approach suitable for children?**

A. The first half of the cedar avenue is flat, but the latter half involves a steep climb with stone steps. It's generally manageable for elementary school-aged children and older. Strollers are not recommended; a baby carrier is advised for younger children.

**Q3. Can I eat Togakushi soba at Chusha Shrine?**

A. There are many famous restaurants around Chusha Shrine, such as "**Uzura-ya,**" "**Nagano,**" and "**Iwato-ya**." Weekends often see long queues, so the trick is to aim for **right after opening**.

**Q4. What about visiting in winter?**

A. The Okusha Shrine approach is often impassable due to snow, and there are periods when only Chusha and Hokosha Shrines are accessible. Please check the official website for opening and closing information in advance.

**Q5. What are the hours for receiving Goshuin?**

A. The shrine offices at each shrine typically offer Goshuin from **9:00 AM to 5:00 PM** (hours may vary by season). There are times when Okusha Shrine's Goshuin is offered as a **pre-written slip at Chusha Shrine**, so please check for the latest information.
