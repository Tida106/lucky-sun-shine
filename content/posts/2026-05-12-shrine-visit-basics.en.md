## Introduction

Visiting a shrine is one of the **most familiar spiritual cultures for Japanese people**. For over a thousand years, it has been an integral part of life, serving as a place to visit for New Year's prayers (Hatsumode), Shichi-Go-San festivals, exorcism of bad luck (Yakubarai), and other significant life events. However, many people feel they **"somehow know but aren't confident"** about things like **correct etiquette, dress code, and how to handle omamori (charms)**. This article compiles everything you need to know on one page, from **basic knowledge and practical manners to recommended shrines for specific wishes, rules for Goshuin (shrine seals) and Omamori, and FAQs**.

## <span id="basics"></span>What is a Shrine? (Basics of Shinto)

Shrines are **sacred places unique to Japan, dedicated to the myriad gods (Yaoyorozu no Kami)**. Shinto is an indigenous Japanese faith based on **"nature worship and ancestor veneration,"** without a founder or scriptures, where **mountains, rivers, rocks, ancient trees, ancestors, and great people** are enshrined as gods.

### Main Types of Shrines

| Type | Example Deities | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Jingu | Emperor, Imperial Ancestors | Highest rank, e.g., Ise Jingu and Meiji Jingu |
| Taisha | Powerful Ancient Deities | E.g., Izumo Taisha and Kasuga Taisha |
| Hachimangu | Hachiman-jin (Emperor Ojin) | God of martial fortune and warding off evil |
| Inari Shrine | Ukanomitama-no-Kami | Prosperity in business and bountiful harvests |
| Tenmangu | Sugawara no Michizane | Academic achievement |

### Differences Between Shrines and Temples

- **Shinto Shrine** — Shinto, enshrines gods, has a torii gate, and involves two bows, two claps, and one bow
- **Buddhist Temple** — Buddhism, enshrines Buddhas, has a sanmon gate, and involves gassho (palms together) and one bow (no clapping)

They are often confused, but be aware that their worship etiquette differs significantly.

## <span id="manner"></span>Correct Etiquette for Shrine Visits

The basic flow of a shrine visit is as follows:

1.  **Bow once before the torii gate** (a sign of entering the sacred area)
2.  **Do not walk in the center of the sando (path)** (the center is the path for the deities)
3.  **Purify yourself at the temizuya (water purification pavilion)**
4.  **Quietly place your offering (saisen)**
5.  **Ring the bell if there is one** (a signal to the deity)
6.  **Perform two bows, two claps, and one bow**
7.  **Bow once again facing the torii gate on your way out**

It is customary to avoid walking in the center of the sando, known as **"seichu,"** to show respect for the deities.

## <span id="temizu"></span>Temizu (Water Purification) Etiquette

Purification at the temizuya is a **simplified version of misogi (ritual purification)** before visiting the shrine. Traditionally, people would purify their bodies in rivers or the sea before standing before the deities.

### Correct Temizu Procedure

1.  Hold the ladle (hishaku) with your right hand, scoop water, and purify your **left hand**.
2.  Switch the ladle to your left hand and purify your **right hand**.
3.  Switch back to your right hand, pour some water into your left palm, and **rinse your mouth** (do not let the ladle touch your mouth directly).
4.  Purify your **left hand** once more.
5.  Hold the ladle upright so that the remaining water runs down the handle, purifying it.
6.  Place the ladle back in its original position, face down.

Ideally, all steps should be completed with **one scoop of water**. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, more shrines have adopted a style where visitors purify themselves with flowing water without using a ladle.

## <span id="bow"></span>Meaning of Two Bows, Two Claps, One Bow

This is the standard form of worship etiquette at shrines. It is **common at almost all shrines**, but some, like Izumo Taisha and Usa Jingu, follow a different etiquette of "**two bows, four claps, one bow**."

### Basic Procedure

1.  Quietly place your offering (do not throw it).
2.  Ring the bell.
3.  **Two bows** (bow deeply 90 degrees twice).
4.  Bring your hands together at chest height, **shift your right hand slightly lower, and clap twice**.
5.  With your hands together, make your wish or express gratitude silently.
6.  **One bow** (bow deeply once).

### Meaning of Two Bows, Two Claps, One Bow

-   **Two Bows** — Deep bows to show respect to the deity.
-   **Two Claps** — To summon the deity and ward off evil spirits.
-   **One Bow** — A bow of gratitude after conveying your wish.

The basic mindset for shrine visits is, "**First gratitude, then your wish**." Focusing on "**thanks**" **and** "**a declaration of resolve**" rather than just making requests aligns better with the spirit of Shinto.

## <span id="dress"></span>What to Bring and Dress Code

### What to Bring

-   **Small change** (for offerings; a 5-yen coin is considered lucky for "go-en" or good connections)
-   **Handkerchief** (for drying hands after temizu)
-   **Goshuincho** (if you collect Goshuin seals)
-   **Old omamori** (if you plan to return them)

### Dress Code

| Occasion | Dress Code Guideline |
|---|---|
| Regular Visit | Clean, casual attire |
| Formal Visit or Prayer | Men: Suit, Women: Jacket |
| Mikagaki-uchi Visit (e.g., Ise Jingu) | Black, navy, or grey attire similar to formal wear |
| Wedding or Shichi-Go-San | Formal wear or kimono |

### Attire to Avoid

-   Excessive exposure (e.g., tank tops, short shorts)
-   Overly flashy colors
-   Hats and sunglasses (remove before the main hall)
-   Excessive perfume

The basic principle is to wear "**attire that you wouldn't be ashamed to wear before the deities**."

## <span id="goshuin"></span>About Goshuin (Shrine Seals)

Goshuin are "**proof of your visit**" — a seal bestowed by shrines, featuring a red stamp and calligraphy. Originally said to have begun in the Heian period as a mark for sutra copying, they are now widely given to visitors.

### Goshuin Etiquette

-   **Always receive it after visiting the shrine** (getting it before is putting the cart before the horse).
-   Traditionally, it's customary to **keep separate Goshuincho for shrines and temples** (though there are various theories).
-   **Wait quietly while it's being written**.
-   Prepare the **hatsuho-ryo (offering, typically 300-500 yen)**; try to have exact change.
-   Treating it like a stamp rally is not appropriate; handle it carefully as a **sacred seal**.

### How to Choose a Goshuincho

-   A **dedicated accordion-fold (jabara-ori) style** is standard.
-   Shrine-original ones have high commemorative value.
-   Available in **large and medium sizes**; choose based on ease of storage.

## <span id="omamori"></span>About Omamori (Charms)

Omamori are "**manifestations of the deities**" bestowed at shrines. It is considered taboo to look inside the bag, so it is proper etiquette to handle them with care.

### How to Handle Omamori

-   **Do not open the contents of the bag**.
-   **Always keep them close to you** (in your bag, wallet, or pocket).
-   **It's fine to have multiple omamori** (Shinto belief is that deities do not compete).
-   **It is traditional to return them to the shrine where they were received after about a year**.
-   If you **cannot return them to the original shrine due to distance**, it is acceptable to place them in the old charm collection box (Kofuda Nōsho) at a nearby shrine.

### Returning Omamori

-   Place old omamori and o-fuda (talisman) in the **"Kofuda Nōsho" or "Kofudasho" (old charm collection box)**.
-   They will be respectfully burned in a sacred purification ceremony (otakiage).
-   Returning them at **Dondoyaki (around January 15th)** is also a tradition.

## <span id="faq"></span>Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. Should I avoid visiting shrines during mourning?**

A. Traditionally, if you are **within two degrees of kinship to the deceased** and within 50 days (kyochu or period of intense mourning), you should avoid passing under a torii gate. After this period (mochu or extended mourning), many shrines allow visits, but some families may choose to refrain from New Year's visits. If you are concerned, please inquire directly with the shrine.

**Q2. Can I visit during menstruation?**

A. In ancient times, there was a tradition of refraining due to "blood impurity," but **most major shrines today have no specific restrictions**. If you are concerned, "yohai" (worshipping from outside the torii gate) is also an option.

**Q3. Is it okay to visit multiple shrines in one day?**

A. **It's perfectly fine**. Pilgrimages to multiple shrines, such as Sansha-mairi (visiting three shrines) or Shichifukujin Meguri (Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage), are ancient traditions. However, visiting too many in one day can dilute your focus, so **around three shrines per day** is recommended.

**Q4. What is the correct amount for an offering?**

A. **There is no set amount**. Amounts preferred for their auspicious wordplay include **5 yen (go-en, meaning 'good connections'), 45 yen (shiju go-en, meaning 'constant good connections'), and 125 yen (jubun go-en, meaning 'ample good connections')**. On the other hand, a **10-yen coin is sometimes avoided because its pronunciation sounds like 'en ga tō-en' (縁が遠縁), meaning 'connections become distant'**. **Your feelings are more important than the amount**.

**Q5. How many wishes can I make?**

A. Traditionally, it's "**Issha Ichigan**" (one wish per shrine). It's said that making too many wishes can disperse the deity's power. **Choosing your single most important wish**, or focusing on gratitude, aligns better with the spirit of Shinto.

**Q6. Is it okay to bring crystals to a shrine?**

A. Yes. Many people bring them with the intention of "**purifying**" **their stones with the sacred energy of the shrine**. Common practices include holding them in your palm before passing under the torii gate or placing them near your omamori or o-fuda after your visit. ## Editorial Team's View: Avoiding Becoming "Etiquette Police"

While we've detailed the etiquette here, the editorial team unanimously agrees that "**sincerity of heart**" **takes precedence over** "**perfect manners**" on site.

Even if you make a mistake with the ladle procedure at temizu or get the order of the two bows, two claps, one bow mixed up, if you approach the main hall with respect, the deities probably won't mind. What's more concerning, in fact, is the phenomenon of "**etiquette police**" on social media, pointing out others' mistakes. That's behavior furthest from the atmosphere of a shrine.

Etiquette is merely a "**framework for centering your feelings**," not an evaluation criterion. If you make a mistake, a slight bow and a fresh start right there are perfectly sufficient.

### Common Misconceptions

-   ❌ "**Specific wishes are more likely to be granted**" → ✅ In Shinto, a "**declaration of resolve**" is traditional. Saying "I will do..." is closer to the original form of prayer than "I want to be...".
-   ❌ "**Larger offerings bring greater blessings**" → ✅ There are no upper or lower limits on the amount. **Sincerity of heart** is what truly matters.
-   ❌ "**Visiting every day until your wish comes true shows devotion**" → ✅ A **moderate approach**, including "Oreimairi" (visits to express thanks), aligns better with the Shinto sensibility.
-   ❌ "**Making multiple wishes at once dilutes their effectiveness**" → ✅ While "Issha Ichigan" (one wish per shrine) is traditional, expressions of gratitude can be conveyed as many times as you like.
