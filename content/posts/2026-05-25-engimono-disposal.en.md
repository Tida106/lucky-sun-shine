## Why You Shouldn't Dispose of Engimono as Regular Trash

Engimono — especially **omamori and ofuda** — are received from shrines and temples as **"embodiments of gods or buddhas (goshinji)."** In traditional Japanese culture, **"discarding a divine embodiment with regular combustible trash" is considered an act of disrespect.**

### Gratitude for What Has Served Its Purpose

Lucky charms like Maneki-neko, Daruma dolls, Kumade (rake charms), and Shichifukujin (Seven Lucky Gods) are like "companions" that have supported your, or your family's, luck since the day you acquired them. Saying **"Thank you for your service and hard work"** and bidding them farewell with gratitude is the essence of Japanese engimono culture.

> **Note**: Disposal etiquette is primarily cultural and symbolic. You will not be "cursed" or "haunted" if you dispose of them as regular trash. However, the **"meaning as a lucky charm" will certainly diminish**, so our editorial team recommends handling them with care whenever possible.

### Consideration for the Environment and Neighbors

In recent years, the **number of engimono made from non-combustible materials (ceramics, metal, glass) has increased**. Therefore, some shrines may limit the types of materials they accept. It's important to remember that not "everything is okay if you put it out for Otakiage," so be aware of material-specific considerations.

### If You Don't Remember When You Received It

If the date you received it is unknown, returning it all together during **New Year's (January)** or on **New Year's Eve** is a clear milestone. Designating one day a year to "review everything" will help prevent them from accumulating in your drawer for years.

## Proper Disposal Methods | 4 Options

There are broadly **four ways** to dispose of engimono. Please use them according to your situation.

### ① Shrine or Temple Kofuda Osamesho (Most Formal)

Nearly all shrines and temples have a **"Kofuda Osamesho (Old Talisman Collection Box)"** or **"Nosatsusho"** where you can return old omamori and ofuda.

-   **Location**: Near the entrance of the shrine grounds, next to the shrine office, etc.
-   **Timing**: Accepted year-round (varies by shrine).
-   **Procedure**: Wrap them in white paper and quietly place them in the collection box.
-   **Gratuity**: Some people offer a monetary donation (saisen) but it's not a rule.
-   **Cost**: Generally free (saisen is optional).

Returning them to the shrine where you received them is the original practice, but **if you cannot visit a distant shrine, nearby shrines will often accept them** (see next section).

### ② Dondo-yaki (Regional Fire Festival)

**Around January 15th** (or sometime between January 7th and 15th, depending on the region), a **Koshogatsu (Little New Year) fire festival** called Dondo-yaki is held at local shrines or open spaces. Old lucky charms, New Year's decorations, and calligraphy are burned together.

-   **Timing**: Around January 7th to 15th (varies by region).
-   **Location**: Local shrines, temples, or community open spaces.
-   **Items that can be brought**: Omamori, ofuda, shimenawa, hamaya, Daruma dolls, other old engimono.
-   **Items that cannot be brought**: Items mixed with plastic, metal, or ceramics (due to environmental impact).
-   **Cost**: Generally free.

The date of the festival is announced in **community newsletters, neighborhood circulars, or on shrine bulletin boards**. You can also find information about nearby events by searching for "**Dondo-yaki [Your City/Town]**."

### ③ Otakiage (Burning Ritual) Services (Mail-in Option)

This is a convenient option for **omamori from distant shrines** or when **there is no Kofuda Osamesho nearby**. Shrines, temples, and specialized companies offer **mail-in Otakiage services**.

| Type | Details | Estimated Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Mail-in Returns to Large Shrines** | Some large shrines like Ise Grand Shrine, Izumo Taisha, and Dazaifu Tenmangu offer official services. | Shipping cost + Otakiage fee (optional) |
| **Specialized Otakiage Services** | Private companies accept items by the box (for individuals). | ¥3,000 - ¥10,000 per box |
| **Estate Clearing Service Option** | Included with moving or estate clearing services. | May be included in service fee. |

Before using, always check the **official website for eligibility, shipping address, and estimated Hatsuhoryo (offering fee)**.

### ④ At-Home Purification (Procedure for Unavoidable Cases)

This is the **minimum proper procedure** if you absolutely cannot visit a shrine or use mail-in services.

#### 5 Steps for At-Home Purification

1.  **Prepare white paper or a white cloth** (rice paper, copier paper, or a handkerchief can work).
2.  **Wrap the omamori or engimono**.
3.  **Sprinkle a pinch of salt** over it (for purification).
4.  **Put your hands together and say "Thank you very much"** to express gratitude.
5.  On the **combustible trash day**, place it in a **separate bag** from other trash (a small, separate bag).

#### For Non-Combustible Items

Ceramic Maneki-neko, omamori with metal parts, or glass engimono should be placed in **non-combustible trash**. The procedure is the same as above (wrap in white paper, sprinkle salt, and express gratitude).

Rather than just "throwing it away as trash," the essence of at-home purification is **"performing a farewell ritual before discarding it according to disposal categories."** — This difference in mindset is key.

## What Not to Do: NG Disposal Methods

### ❌ Disposal Methods to Absolutely Avoid

-   **Throwing them into general trash without any ritual** — Lacks respect as a lucky charm.
-   **Burning them in your home garden or on a stove because they are combustible** — Violation of fire safety regulations, potential neighbor disputes, and smoke pollution.
-   **Discarding them into rivers, seas, or lakes** — Contributes to environmental pollution and constitutes illegal dumping.
-   **Burying them in the ground (on one's own judgment)** — Can be illegal dumping depending on the location.
-   **Selling them on flea market apps or at recycling shops** — Omamori are embodiments of deities; reselling them is a breach of etiquette.
-   **Giving them to others** — Omamori received for oneself are generally meant for individual use.
-   **Leaving them in a drawer for many years** — Long-term storage in a "duty-completed" state is believed to stagnate energy.

### ❌ What Not to Do When Bringing to a Shrine

-   **Bringing a large quantity of items from other shrines for Otakiage** — May not be accepted; check in advance.
-   **Silently mixing in non-combustible materials** — Becomes a burden on shrine priests and the community.
-   **Including perishable goods or food (like kagamimochi)** — Not subject to Otakiage.
-   **Mixing personal items like photos or mementos** — Requires separate, specialized memorial services.

### ❌ What Not to Do at Dondo-yaki

-   **Throwing in items containing plastic, vinyl, or metal** — Environmental burden.
-   **Bringing large items (e.g., extra-large Daruma dolls)** — Confirm with the organizers in advance.
-   **Offerings other than sake or sweets** — Fire hazard.

## What to Do with Omamori from Distant Shrines?

Omamori received while traveling, or ofuda received from a distant shrine during a child's Shichi-Go-San celebration—these cases often cause confusion about where to return them.

### Solution Flowchart

| Situation | Recommended Action |
|---|---|
| **Can revisit** (e.g., travel plans within the year) | Bring to the Kofuda Osamesho of the shrine where it was received. |
| **Cannot revisit, but the shrine offers mail-in service** | Use the official website for mail-in return. |
| **No mail-in service available** | **Bring to a nearby shrine's Kofuda Osamesho** (Shinto generally accepts items from different sects). |
| **No shrine nearby / No time** | Use a specialized Otakiage service. |
| **None of the above are feasible** | At-home purification (white paper + salt + gratitude). |

### Basis for "It's Okay Even if the Shrine is Different"

In Shinto, the fundamental concept is that **"the eight million gods do not contend with each other."** Even if the shrine where you received the charm and the shrine where you return it are different, the Otakiage ritual itself is performed sacredly. However, **"returning it to the place where it was received" is the original practice**. Strive for courtesy within feasible limits.

### What About Temple Charms?

Omamori received from temples (Buddhism) should **preferably be returned to a temple** as the original practice. Many shrines' Kofuda Osamesho will accept them, but it's **best to confirm in advance**.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. I found a large number of old omamori from years ago. What should I do?**

A. You can **return them all together to a Kofuda Osamesho** without any problem. They will accept them no matter how many years have passed. If you feel uneasy, you can apologize in your heart, saying, "**I apologize for leaving them unattended for so long**," before returning them. It's okay to bundle multiple items together in white paper before placing them.

**Q2. Is "money" required for Otakiage?**

A. **It is generally free**, but it's common etiquette to offer a **Saisen (monetary donation) of about ¥500 to ¥3,000** as an Otakiage fee or Hatsuhoryo (offering fee) (it's not mandatory). For mail-in Otakiage services, the typical cost is ¥3,000 to ¥10,000 per box.

**Q3. What if there's no Dondo-yaki in my area?**

A. You have three options: ① Return them to the **regular Kofuda Osamesho** of a nearby shrine, ② Search for nearby events by looking up "**Dondo-yaki [Your City/Town]**" or "**Sagicho [Your Prefecture]**," or ③ Use a **mail-in Otakiage service**. Around January 15th, many shrines' Kofuda Osamesho will also increase their acceptance of items.

**Q4. Can I dispose of pet charms, stuffed animals, or photos together with lucky charms?**

A. **Generally, please handle them separately.** Pet items should go to **animal cemeteries or shrines for pet memorial services**, stuffed animals to **doll memorial services**, and photos to **photo memorial services**. It is considered polite to avoid bringing them to a Kofuda Osamesho along with omamori.

**Q5. What's the difference between "Otakiage" and "Dondo-yaki"?**

A. **"Otakiage"** is a general term for sacred rituals performed by shrines and temples **year-round or periodically** that involve burning. **"Dondo-yaki"** (also known as **Sagicho**) is a fire festival held by local communities during **Koshogatsu (around January 15th)**, and it is a type of Otakiage. We recommend using **"Dondo-yaki" for New Year's decorations** as is traditional, and **"shrine Kofuda Osamesho → Otakiage" for regular omamori and ofuda**.

## Editorial Team's Perspective: Disposal as a Ritual to Create a "Boundary"

Regarding the disposal of engimono, the editorial team honestly believes that **disposal rituals are as valuable for "creating a boundary for oneself" as they are for "respecting deities."**

The moment you return a year-old omamori to the Kofuda Osamesho, you naturally reflect on **"how I spent this past year."** Whether your wishes came true or not, expressing gratitude and saying goodbye — this boundary is the impetus for starting the next year with a fresh mindset.

### Editorial Team's Recommendation: "Annual Engimono Reset Day"

Creating an **"Engimono Review Day"** on the same date each year will prevent them from accumulating in your drawer.

| Timing | Content |
|---|---|
| **New Year's Eve ~ New Year's Day** | Return the year's omamori to Kofuda Osamesho. Get new omamori for the New Year. |
| **Setsubun (around February 3rd)** | Renew your amulet for warding off evil and other lucky charms. |
| **Dondo-yaki (around January 15th)** | Return them together with shimenawa and New Year's decorations. |
| **Birthday** | Reset personal omamori you've worn for a year. |
| **Milestones like moving or changing jobs** | Clear out charms when your environment changes. |

It's easy to keep putting off disposal, thinking, "**I can still use it**" or "**It's a waste**," and your drawer can become a graveyard for lucky charms. Deciding to **draw a line after one year** actually makes it easier to invite new fortune.

### 3 Phrases to Center Your Feelings During Disposal

Before returning them to the Kofuda Osamesho, or before wrapping them in white paper for at-home purification, try reciting these three phrases in your heart:

1.  **"Thank you for this past year."** (Gratitude)
2.  **"Thank you for watching over me, and for your hard work."** (Appreciation)
3.  **"For the next year too, I ask for new good fortune."** (Aspiration for the future)

Just doing this transforms disposal from **"taking out the trash" to a "ritual."**
