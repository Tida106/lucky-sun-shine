## What is Kumade? From Agricultural Tool to a Lucky Charm that "Rakes in Fortune"

A Kumade was originally an **agricultural tool used to gather fallen leaves and rice stalks**. Its name, which means "bear's hand," comes from its shape, which resembles a bear's paw with widely spread claws at the end of a long handle.

### Transformation into a Lucky Charm

During the Edo period, from the association of "**quickly raking in a pile of good fortune**," agricultural Kumade began to be decorated with auspicious ornaments like **koban (old Japanese gold coins), rice bales, sea bream, Otafuku masks, treasure ships, and pine, bamboo, and plum blossoms**. These "**Engi Kumade**" (auspicious Kumade) then became popular among merchants.

### Tori-no-ichi Market and Kumade

Inseparable from Kumade is the **Tori-no-ichi (Rooster Market)**. This is a festival held mainly in the Kanto region on the **Day of the Rooster in November**. During this festival, Kumade vendors line the shrine grounds, and visitors purchase Kumade, wishing for good fortune in the coming year.

-   **"Ichino-Tori," "Nino-Tori," "Sanno-Tori"** — There are usually two to three Days of the Rooster in November.
-   **"Years with three Tori-no-ichi days often see many fires"** — a traditional saying passed down from the Edo period (no scientific basis).
-   **Originating Shrine** — Ohtori Shrine in Adachi Ward, Tokyo, is considered the birthplace of Tori-no-ichi.
-   **Famous Tori-no-ichi in Tokyo** — Asakusa's Otori Shrine, Shinjuku's Hanazono Shrine, Fuchu's Okunitama Shrine, among others.

### Is Tori-no-ichi only in Kanto?

Tori-no-ichi is a festival **primarily held in the Kanto region**, with fewer equivalent Kumade markets in Kansai. However, in recent years, some shrines in **Osaka, Kyoto, and Nagoya** have also started hosting them. Check your local shrine's annual events calendar!

## Buying Etiquette: Haggling, Gratuities, and Hand-Clapping Rituals

Purchasing a Kumade involves a **unique "ritual" not found with other lucky charms**. Knowing these customs can significantly enhance your Tori-no-ichi experience.

### ① Haggling is Good Manners

Kumade are rare lucky charms where "**haggling is considered good luck**." You negotiate with the vendor from the initial asking price to reach a mutually agreeable price.

### ② It's Sophisticated to Give the Haggled Difference as a Gratuity

However—it's **not just about haggling down the price and being done**. The sophisticated Edo-style custom is to **hand the difference you haggled off as a "gratuity" to the vendor**.

#### Example

-   Initial price: **¥10,000**
-   After negotiation: Agreed on **¥8,000**
-   Payment: **You pay ¥10,000 and say "Go-shugi!" (Gratuity!) while handing the ¥2,000 difference back to the vendor.**

This is a culture unique to Kumade, serving both as a "**good luck charm for business prosperity**" and a "**token of congratulations to the vendor**." The Edo business philosophy of "**starting the year feeling good about each other**" is still alive today.

### ③ The "Shanshan" Hand-Clapping Ritual (Tejime)

Once the price is agreed upon, the vendor, buyer, and surrounding people perform a "**Tejime**" (hand-clapping ritual).

-   **Sanbonjime** ("Three-claps"): "Shashashan, shashashan, shashashan, shan" (the most formal)
-   **Ipponjime** ("One-clapping"): "Shashashan, shashashan, shashashan, shan" (a simplified version)
-   **Icchojime** ("One-strike"): "Shan" (for when time is short)

When the vendor calls out, "**Ote o haishaku!**" (Let's have a round of applause!), everyone around joins in clapping to celebrate. This is a famous sight that creates the lively atmosphere of Tori-no-ichi. **If you're a beginner, just clap along with the vendor's call; you'll be fine!**

### ④ How to Carry It Home

The tradition is to **carry your newly purchased Kumade held higher than your face**. This is a good luck ritual to "**bring home all the fortune without spilling any**." This can be tricky on trains or in taxis, so for those close to the Tori-no-ichi, **walking home is the original custom**.

## The Custom of Getting a Bigger One Each Year

The **most significant feature** of Kumade is the custom of **continually buying a slightly larger size from the same vendor each year**.

### Symbol of Business Prosperity and Expansion

-   Year 1: **Small size (¥3,000)**
-   Year 2: **Small-medium size (¥5,000)**
-   Year 3: **Medium size (¥8,000)**
-   ...and so on, as your business grows, your Kumade also grows.

It's important to "**buy from the same vendor**." This deepens your connection with the merchant each year, and some vendors even become like family. Vendors often keep customer records, and exchanging greetings like, "Long time no see! Please take care of me again this year" is a familiar scene at Tori-no-ichi.

### If Sizing Up is Difficult

-   **Keep the same size but make the decorations more elaborate.**
-   **Mini sizes are sufficient for sole proprietors and households** (no need to force a size increase).
-   **You can also downsize** if you relocate or close your business.

The essence of Kumade culture is to "**continue for a long time without overextending yourself**."

## How to Display It: Orientation, Location, and Height

### Basic Display Locations

| Location                    | Recommendation | Reason                                        |
| :-------------------------- | :------------- | :-------------------------------------------- |
| **Behind the cash register/counter in a shop** | ◎              | Traditional spot for business prosperity      |
| **Entrance of a company/office** | ◎              | Position to welcome good fortune              |
| **Entrance of your home**   | ○              | Invites good fortune into the entire house    |
| **Next to a kamidana (household Shinto altar)** | ○              | Close to deities, showing respect             |
| **East or south side of the living room** | ○              | Receives positive "Yang" energy               |

### Correct Orientation

-   Face the **front of the Kumade** (the decorated side) **towards the entrance** — imagining it raking in incoming good fortune.
-   **In a shop, position it where it's visible from the entrance**; at home, where it's visible from the front door.
-   Display it in a **high place** (above eye level) — treating it with respect, similar to deities.
-   **Facing East or South** — directions that invite positive energy.

### Display Etiquette

-   **Above eye level** (treated with the same respect as a kamidana)
-   **Avoid direct sunlight** — to prevent damage to paper and bamboo decorations.
-   **Avoid humid and poorly ventilated areas.**
-   **Dust it regularly** — lightly with a duster.

### Display Methods to Avoid

-   **Displaying it upside down** — symbolizes fortune falling away.
-   **Facing the front of the Kumade towards a wall** — unable to rake in good fortune.
-   **Placing it directly on the floor** — inappropriate for a lucky charm.
-   **In front of a toilet, bathroom, or kitchen sink** — water is believed to wash away fortune.

## Disposal Methods: Traditional Exchange at Next Year's Tori-no-ichi

Kumade are traditionally **exchanged annually**. Old Kumade are **returned to the same vendor at the next year's Tori-no-ichi**, and a new one is purchased.

### Three Disposal Options

| Method                          | Details                                                               |
| :------------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Exchange at next year's Tori-no-ichi** | Most traditional. Give it to the same vendor and buy a new one. |
| **Shrine's old charm collection box** | At Tori-no-ichi hosting shrines or nearby shrines. It will be ritually burned (otakiage). |
| **Don-doyaki**                   | Regional fire festival around January 15th.                          |

### Home Disposal

If you must dispose of it at home:

1.  **Wrap it in white paper.**
2.  **Sprinkle a pinch of salt on it.**
3.  **Express gratitude, saying "Thank you very much."**
4.  **Dispose of it on non-burnable trash day, separated from other garbage** (due to numerous metal parts).

### Is It Okay to Display an Old Kumade for More Than a Year?

Kumade are traditionally believed to "**absorb fortune for one year and then complete their purpose**," so **continuing to display an old one is not recommended for good luck**. However, some families keep **special Kumade with sentimental value**, so it's not an absolute rule.

## How to Choose Without Failure and What to Avoid

### ✅ Checklist for Choosing

1.  **Does it have decorations that match your wishes?** — Koban for financial luck, sea bream and rice bales for business.
2.  **Does the size fit your display space?** — Avoid buying one that's too big to display.
3.  **The personality of the Kumade vendor** — Choose a vendor you'd like to buy from again next year.
4.  **Quality of decorations** — Cheaper items may fade or tear quickly.
5.  **Weight and balance** — An overly large Kumade can be difficult to carry home and display.

### ❌ What to Avoid

-   **Starting with an extra large Kumade in the first year** — Makes it impossible to size up in subsequent years.
-   **Buying from multiple vendors in the same year** — Thought to disperse your luck.
-   **Buying at the asking price without haggling** — Considered disrespectful to the vendor (it doesn't show an understanding of the custom).
-   **Pocketing the haggled difference, thinking it's a "bargain"** — Shows a lack of understanding of the gratuity culture.
-   **Displaying it upside down or facing a wall** — Prevents it from raking in good fortune.
-   **Not replacing it with a new one after a year** — Fortune is believed to become stale.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. I'm a beginner who's never been to Tori-no-ichi. What time is it usually less crowded?**

A. **Weekdays mornings** are usually less crowded. **Evenings and late nights** are the busiest, so use your discretion: go in the evening if you want to experience the lively atmosphere, or in the morning if you prefer a calmer shopping experience. If there are **"Sanno-Tori" (three Tori-no-ichi days) in a year**, you'll have three chances, making it easier to avoid crowds.

**Q2. I'm not good at haggling... Is it rude to buy at the asking price?**

A. **Buying at the asking price is not rude at all.** Vendors are usually kind to beginners. If you're thinking, "I want to try haggling but I'm not confident," it's perfectly fine to state a total price like, "**Please accept this amount, including a gratuity**." What's important is creating an atmosphere where "**everyone starts the year feeling good**."

**Q3. Is it okay to display Kumade with other lucky charms (like Maneki Neko or Daruma)?**

A. **Absolutely no problem!** In fact, it's traditional for shops to display a **three-piece set: Kumade, Maneki Neko, and Daruma**. However, your intentions will be clearer if you **keep a consistent theme** (e.g., all for business prosperity). For more details, refer to [A Complete Guide to Choosing Lucky Items](/blog/lucky-items-guide/).

**Q4. Does buying a Kumade have meaning for individuals (families) who don't run a business?**

A. **Yes, it certainly does!** Even if you don't run a business, Kumade blessings apply to households for things like "**inviting family fortune,**" "**financial stability,**" and "**children's growth**." Starting with a **mini size (¥1,000–¥3,000)** is recommended.

**Q5. Can I buy a Kumade if I live in a region without a nearby Tori-no-ichi?**

A. **Yes, you can buy them online** (e.g., Amazon and Rakuten). However, since you **cannot experience the rituals of haggling, gratuities, and hand-clapping** through online shopping, **visiting a famous Tori-no-ichi as part of a trip** is one way to enjoy it. Some families even incorporate it as a once-a-year travel event.

## Editorial Team's View: Kumade as a Device to Reaffirm the "Connection with Vendors" Annually

Speaking frankly about Kumade, our editorial team believes that "**the true essence of Kumade is less about being a decorative lucky charm and more about being a mechanism to reaffirm the connection with a vendor each year**."

Buying a slightly larger Kumade from the same vendor each year, giving a gratuity, and celebrating with Tejime—this series of rituals is a modern embodiment of the merchant philosophy that "**business cannot be done alone; it only flourishes with the support of others**."
