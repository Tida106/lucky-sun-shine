## What is Maneki Neko? A Unique Japanese Lucky Charm Since the Edo Period

Maneki Neko (まねきねこ) is a **cat figurine with a raised paw, beckoning people and good fortune.** It is a **unique Japanese lucky charm** said to have originated in the late Edo period, and it is now cherished worldwide as the "Lucky Cat" or "Fortune Cat."

### Multiple Theories on its Origin

| Origin Theory | Content |
|---|---|
| **Gotokuji Temple Theory** (Setagaya, Tokyo) | In the Edo period, Ii Naotaka took shelter from a thunderstorm after being beckoned by a white cat at the temple, leading the Ii family to support the temple. It is known as the birthplace of Maneki Neko. |
| **Imado Shrine Theory** (Asakusa, Tokyo) | An old woman in the Edo period, impoverished, had to let go of her beloved cat. The cat appeared in her dream and advised her to make and sell "cat dolls," which is said to be the origin. |
| **Jishoin Temple Theory** (Shinjuku, Tokyo) | Ota Dokan, lost in battle, was guided by a black cat to victory. He enshrined the black cat, which is said to be the beginning. |

There is no "correct" answer, and these theories coexist. What they share is the belief that they originated from experiences where **"a cat saved people or invited good fortune."**

## Types and How to Choose: Meanings Change with Paw Position, Color, and Size

### Paw Position: Right Paw, Left Paw, Both Paws

**The most important point about Maneki Neko is which paw it has raised.** The meanings are distinctly different.

| Paw Raised | What it Invites | Suitable Place or Person |
|---|---|---|
| **Right Paw Raised** (often male cats) | **Financial luck, money** | General households not running a business, people focusing on saving money |
| **Left Paw Raised** (often female cats) | **People, customers** | Shops, restaurants, hospitality businesses, people wanting new connections |
| **Both Paws Raised** | Both financial luck and people | Special places like shop entrances. However, some consider it **"giving up" or "surrender,"** so opinions vary. |

### The "Height" of the Raised Paw Also Has Meaning

-   **Raised higher than the ear** → Invites fortune from afar (long-distance, big opportunities, large sums of money)
-   **Raised lower than the ear** → Invites nearby fortune (daily life, small blessings, regular customers)

Shops tend to choose those **with paws raised high.**

## How to Choose Without Mistakes and What to Avoid

### ✅ Checklist for Choosing

1.  **Narrow down your purpose to one** — Being greedy with "financial luck, love luck, and academic success" can dilute the meaning.
2.  **Decide on the placement first** — Check if the size and color style suit the location.
3.  **Check the facial expression** — Even the same product can have individual differences. Choose one with a **calm, gentle smile** to foster a long-lasting affection.
4.  **Check the paw height** — The actual item might look different from the photo.
5.  **Check for cracks or chips** — Ensure there are no defects from shipping.

### ❌ What to Avoid

-   **Casually choosing a Maneki Neko with both paws raised** — Some people avoid this as it can symbolize "giving up," so understand the meaning first.
-   **Cluttering multiple Maneki Neko together** — Limiting to 1 or 2 helps prevent energy dispersion.
-   **Leaving a broken Maneki Neko around** — Once its role is over, it should be respectfully disposed of.
-   **Placing it in watery areas like toilets or bathrooms** — This is believed to disturb the flow of positive energy.
-   **Suddenly gifting one for a birthday or anniversary** — Ask for the recipient's preference to avoid a mismatch.
-   **Replacing it after only a few days because "it's not working"** — It's traditional for lucky charms to be displayed for at least a year.

### Tips for Displaying

-   Place it **slightly below eye level** to avoid an overpowering feel.
-   Facing its gaze **east or south** is believed to invite positive energy (Feng Shui).
-   **Avoid placing it directly opposite a mirror at the entrance** — this arrangement is thought to bounce away good fortune.
-   **Wipe it regularly** — Dust is believed to obstruct the flow of energy.
-   **Accompany it with seasonal flowers or a small plate** — A sign of treating it with care.

### Places You Shouldn't Put Them

-   **Toilet, bathroom, or above the kitchen sink** — Good fortune is believed to be washed away by the water flow.
-   **Directly above a TV or microwave** — Electromagnetic waves are thought to disturb its energy.
-   **Directly on the floor** — A "stepped-on" position is unsuitable for lucky charms.
-   **Dark or humid places** — Believed to lower its energy.

## Care and Disposal Methods

### Daily Care

-   **Dust with a soft, dry cloth**: Weekly is a good guideline.
-   **If visibly dirty, gently wipe with a cloth dampened with lukewarm water**: For ceramic and unglazed items, water washing may be unsuitable, so check beforehand.
-   **If cracked or chipped**: Dispose of it respectfully (see next section).

### Disposal and Memorial Etiquette

In Japan, it's traditional to **"return a Maneki Neko with gratitude once its role is fulfilled."** It's considered bad manners to simply throw it away with regular trash.

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| **Shrine's old charm return box** | It can be respectfully burned in an O-fuda-takigage ceremony (check if they accept Maneki Neko, as some might not). |
| **Dondo-yaki** | A bonfire festival held around January 15th at local shrines. |
| **Maneki Neko memorial service** | Gotokuji Temple, Imado Shrine, and other shrines associated with its origin hold special Maneki Neko memorial services (usually once a year). |
| **Home memorial service** | Wrap it in white paper or cloth, sprinkle a pinch of salt, express gratitude by saying "Thank you very much," and then dispose of it separately from other trash. |

**"Replacing with a new Maneki Neko"** is also a natural act for a lucky charm. Many households switch them out every one to several years.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. Is it okay to display multiple Maneki Neko figurines together?**

A. **It's perfectly fine for up to 2 or 3 figurines.** However, combining **Maneki Neko with different roles**, such as a "right paw for financial luck" and a "left paw for love luck," makes their meanings clearer. Arranging multiple Maneki Neko of the same purpose and color tends to turn them into a "collection" rather than having their energy amplify, diminishing their significance as lucky charms.

**Q2. Is it okay to use a second-hand or gifted Maneki Neko?**

A. **Generally, yes, it's fine.** Unlike amulets, Maneki Neko are not considered "avatars of gods," so there are no major taboos against inheriting them. However, **some schools of thought advise against cracked or heavily soiled second-hand items**, as they are believed to carry over the previous owner's fortune.

**Q3. Is it a good souvenir for people overseas?**

A. **It's a highly recommended souvenir!** They are known worldwide as "Lucky Cats" and are appreciated as a unique Japanese lucky charm. Explaining the meaning of the paw position and color will make it an even more valuable gift. Mini sizes (3-5 cm) are convenient for travel.

**Q4. Is it okay to display Maneki Neko with other lucky charms (e.g., Daruma, Seven Lucky Gods)?**

A. **Yes, it's perfectly fine.** In Japan, it's traditionally common to display multiple lucky charms together. However, it's important **not to clutter the shelf.** Organize them by theme or limit them to 3-5 items to maintain a clear flow of energy. For more details, refer to [Complete Guide to Choosing Lucky Items](/blog/lucky-items-guide/).

**Q5. What should I do if I don't feel any effect?**

A. Resetting is recommended, such as ① trying a different location (e.g., from the entryway to the living room), ② cleaning it to tidy up the energy, or ③ replacing it with a new one after a year. The healthiest approach is to think that **"a lifestyle conscious of Maneki Neko arranges your luck," rather than "Maneki Neko changes your luck."**
