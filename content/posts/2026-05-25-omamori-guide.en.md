## What is Omamori? A Japanese Culture of Carrying a "Piece of the Deity"

Omamori (御守) are **small bags or talismans granted at Shinto shrines and Buddhist temples, symbolizing the protection of the gods and Buddhas**. In Japan, they have long been cherished since the Heian period as an item to **"receive divine protection at all times."**

### You "Receive" (授かる), Not "Buy"

At shrines and temples, Omamori are **"received" (sazukaru)** rather than "bought." The money paid is called **"Hatsuhoryo" (初穂料)** at shrines or **"Ofuse"** at temples, and traditionally, it is regarded not as a price for the item, but as **"an expression of gratitude to the deities."**

### Difference from Ofuda (御札)

| Item | Omamori | Ofuda |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Small (5-10cm) | Large (15-30cm) |
| Shape | Pouch, card, strap, etc. | Vertical paper or wooden board |
| How to use | Worn on the body or carried around | Enshrined on a kamidana (household altar) or specific place in the home |
| Purpose | Personal protection | Protection for the entire house or room |

Many households use both.

### Why You Shouldn't Look Inside an Omamori

Inside an Omamori pouch, there is a **"Goshinjyu" (御神璽)** of the shrine's deity—a small amulet, stone, or piece of cloth. This is the dwelling place of the deity, and **opening the pouch to look inside is considered taboo**. There is a tradition that if you open the pouch, the deity might leave.

## How to Choose Wisely and What to Avoid

### ✅ Checklist for Choosing Omamori

1.  **Narrow down your purpose to one or two** — Buying too many with "I want everything" dilutes your focus.
2.  **Decide where you'll carry it beforehand** — Pouch type for bags, card type for wallets.
3.  **Ensure consistency between the shrine/temple's sect and your wish** — For example, Izumo Taisha for matchmaking, Tenmangu shrines for academics.
4.  **Personal design preference** — Since you'll carry it daily, choose a design you genuinely like.
5.  **Receive a new one** — Used items are believed not to possess the deity's power.

### ❌ What to Avoid (NG Examples)

-   **Opening the pouch to check the contents** — This is the biggest taboo, as it is believed the deity might leave.
-   **Purchasing used Omamori on flea market apps, etc.** — This is considered bad manners and is discouraged by shrine authorities.
-   **Treating it carelessly (throwing it, discarding it, washing it in a washing machine)** — This shows disrespect to "a piece of the deity."
-   **Leaving it for a long period (several years) without returning it** — Traditionally, they are replaced after one year.
-   **Stuffing Omamori from different religious facilities into the same place** — It is more respectful to keep Shinto, Buddhist, and Christian items separate.
-   **Placing it near toilets or trash cans** — Avoid impure locations.

### Proper Rules for Carrying Multiple Omamori

There's a common saying that "deities will quarrel if you carry multiple Omamori," but **in Shinto, it's believed that "deities do not quarrel,"** and carrying multiple Omamori has been traditionally acceptable.

However, practically speaking:

-   **Up to 3 to 5 Omamori** is the upper limit to avoid dispersing your focus.
-   **Carrying multiple Omamori for the same purpose has little meaning** (e.g., carrying three matchmaking Omamori won't triple the effect).
-   **Combining different purposes** is more meaningful (e.g., good fortune and warding off evil and health).
-   Ultimately, the best upper limit is **only as many as you can treat with care**.

| Location | Merits | Cautions |

### Caution for Online Offerings

In recent years, some large shrines like **Ise Grand Shrine, Izumo Grand Shrine, and Dazaifu Tenmangu** accept **Omamori offerings via mail** through their official websites. This is a valid option if visiting in person is difficult, but **always apply through the official website**.

Searching for "Omamori" on Rakuten or Amazon may show a mix of resold shrine offerings and private products. It is a golden rule not to buy Omamori from unofficial sources.

## The Correct Way to Carry and Wear Omamori

### Basic Carrying Methods

| Wish | How to Carry |
|---|---|
| **General good fortune and warding off evil** | Always in your everyday bag or wallet |
| **Academic achievement** | School bag, pencil case, between reference books |
| **Financial prosperity** | Inside your wallet (in the same compartment as bills) |
| **Health and recovery from illness** | Worn on the body (around the neck, in an inner pocket) |
| **Traffic safety** | Car keys, motorcycle keys, dashboard |
| **Matchmaking** | Everyday bag, or on a dressing table |
| **Safe delivery** | Mother and child health handbook case, maternity belt |

### Manners When Wearing Omamori

-   **"Always keep it close to your body" is the basic principle** — As close to yourself as possible.
-   **If carrying multiple, keep them in individual bags or pouches** — To prevent them from getting mixed up.
-   **If hanging around your neck, thread the string through the pouch** — Do not remove the contents.
-   **If placing in a car, on the dashboard or ceiling** — Not on the floor or under the seat.

### When Storing at Home

-   **Next to your Kamidana (household altar) if you have one.**
-   **On a clean shelf above eye level.**
-   **Avoid direct sunlight and humidity.**
-   **Placing it on a white cloth** is even more respectful.

## Care and Returning After One Year

### Daily Care

-   **Basically no special care needed** — As it's a piece of the deity, people shouldn't touch it too much.
-   **If it gets dirty, wipe it gently with a soft cloth** — Do not wash with water.
-   **If the string frays, tie it neatly again.**

### Ritual for Returning Omamori After One Year

It is a Japanese tradition to **return Omamori after one year**. It is believed that the deity's power completes its "duty" after a year, and it's time to replace it with a new Omamori.

| Where to Return | Details |
|---|---|
| **Old Omamori/Ofuda drop-off point at the shrine/temple where you received it** | Most formal. Returning it to where you received it is the original practice. |
| **Old Omamori/Ofuda drop-off point at a nearby shrine** | If you cannot return it to the original location, nearby shrines may accept it (varies by shrine). |
| **Dondo-yaki (New Year's bonfire ritual)** | Held around January 15th at local shrines. |
| **During the first three days of the New Year to Matsunouchi (around January 7th)** | A period when old Omamori and Ofuda are intensively returned. |
| **Return by mail** | Some large shrines accept returns by mail (check in advance). |

### Etiquette When Returning

-   **Place it quietly in the old Omamori/Ofuda drop-off point** (some people add a small offering, but it's not a rule).
-   **Express your gratitude silently with "Thank you very much."**
-   **Multiple Omamori can be returned at once** (separating them by shrine or temple is more respectful).
-   **For Dondo-yaki, traditionally place them before noon.**

### Home Consecration/Disposal if You Cannot Return

Minimum etiquette if you absolutely cannot visit a shrine:

1.  **Wrap it in white paper or cloth.**
2.  **Sprinkle a pinch of salt on it.**
3.  **Express your gratitude with "Thank you very much."**
4.  **Dispose of it as burnable waste, separate from other trash.**

Never simply throw it into a regular trash bag.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. Is it okay to carry Omamori from multiple shrines together?**

A. **Yes, it's perfectly fine**. In Shinto, it is believed that "**the numerous deities do not quarrel**," and carrying Omamori from multiple shrines has been practiced since ancient times. However, **don't simply stuff them into the same pouch**, and **3 to 5 Omamori** is a practical upper limit. Omamori from shrines and temples (Shinto and Buddhism) can also be carried together.

**Q2. What should I do if I lose an Omamori?**

A. The traditional interpretation is to **thank it for "having acted as your substitute"** and averted misfortune. Don't be disheartened; it's recommended to start anew by receiving a new Omamori. There's no need to worry that "you won't be protected if you lose it."

**Q3. Is it okay to receive an Omamori for a friend or family member and give it to them?**

A. **Yes, it's perfectly fine**. Gifting Omamori is a custom that has been practiced since ancient times. The feeling of "I visited the shrine thinking of you" holds value beyond the Omamori itself. However, it's important **not to force an Omamori on someone who doesn't want it**.

**Q4. I accidentally washed my Omamori in the washing machine!**

A. **If its shape isn't ruined, continue using it with gratitude or take it to a shrine to consult them**. If it's ruined and the contents have come out, the respectful approach is to **wrap it in white paper and place it in the shrine's old Omamori/Ofuda drop-off point**. Consider it as having "acted as your substitute" and start anew with a new Omamori.

**Q5. How powerful are the blessings of Omamori?**

A. The effectiveness of Omamori holds **cultural and symbolic significance**, and it is not medically or scientifically guaranteed. However, the effect that "**carrying an Omamori aligns one's actions and state of mind**" is somewhat supported psychologically. The sense of security that "a deity is watching over me" can, as a result, change one's behavior—this is the most healthy way Omamori work.
