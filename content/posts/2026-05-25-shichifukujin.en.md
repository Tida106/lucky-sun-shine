## What Are the Seven Lucky Gods? | "The World's Most Open-Minded Divine Team" Born in the Muromachi Period

The Seven Lucky Gods (Shichifukujin) are a collective term for seven deities believed to bring good fortune and prosperity. Their origin is said to date back to the late Muromachi period (around the 15th century), when a Zen monk in Kyoto gathered the main objects of worship of the time and compiled them into the auspicious number "seven."

### Diverse Origins of the Seven Deities

| Deity | Origin Country | Original Religion |
|---|---|---|
| Ebisu | **Japan** | Shinto (believed to be the child of Izanagi and Izanami) |
| Daikokuten | **India** | Hinduism (incarnation of Shiva) 竊・Buddhism |
| Bishamonten | **India** | Buddhism (Northern Guardian of the Four Heavenly Kings) |
| Benzaiten | **India** | Hinduism (Saraswati) 竊・Buddhism |
| Fukurokuju | **China** | Taoism (incarnation of the South Pole Star) |
| Jurojin | **China** | Taoism (Old Man of the South Pole Star) |
| Hotei | **China** | Zen Buddhism (a real Zen monk, Qici/Keishi) |

Deities from Japan, India, and China riding together on a treasure ship窶杯his is a globally rare characteristic of the Seven Lucky Gods. This open-minded form of worship emerged from the Japanese concept of "Yaoyorozu no Kami" (eight million gods), which naturally embraced even foreign deities.

### Widely Embraced by Common People from Muromachi to Edo Periods

During the Edo period, a custom became established where "sleeping with a picture of the Seven Lucky Gods on a treasure ship under your pillow on New Year's Eve will bring a good first dream of the year (Hatsuyume)." This practice took deep root among the common people as a way to invite good luck for the first dream, and it continues to this day.

## The Seven Deities: Their Stories and Blessings

### 竭 Ebisu

| Item | Content |

| **Possessions** | Fishing rod and a sea bream (Tai) |
| **Appearance** | Wearing an eboshi (traditional cap), with a joyful, prosperous expression |

| **Origin** | An ancient Japanese deity. Believed to originate from Hiruko, a child of Izanagi and Izanami |

The **only deity of Japanese origin**. He is the most approachable as the guardian deity of merchants, and festivals such as **"Ebisu-ko" and "Toka Ebisu"** are still held nationwide today.

### 竭｡ Daikokuten

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| **Possessions** | Mallet of luck (Uchide no Kozuchi), a large bag, and bales of rice |

| **Blessings** | **Rich harvest, financial fortune, and prosperity of descendants** |
| **Origin** | Incarnation of Shiva (Mahakala) from India. Syncretized with Okuninushi-no-Mikoto in Japan |

He has been worshipped since ancient times as the **guardian deity of the kitchen** and is widely popular among farmers, merchants, and general households. Often called "**Daikoku-sama**," he symbolizes the head of the household.

| Item | Content |

| **Possessions** | Spear and a treasure pagoda |

| **Origin** | A Buddhist deity from India. Known as Tamonten, the Northern Guardian of the Four Heavenly Kings |

Famous for being worshipped by **Sengoku period warlord Kenshin Uesugi**. As the **only warrior god among the Seven Lucky Gods**, he is popular among **athletes and business leaders**.

The **only goddess among the Seven Lucky Gods**. As the deity of arts and music, she is popular among **entertainers, artists, and students preparing for exams**. The Three Great Benzaiten shrines in Japan are **Enoshima Shrine, Itsukushima Shrine, and Chikubu Island Shrine**.

As his name suggests, he is the **deity who presides over "Fuku (good fortune), Roku (wealth), and Ju (longevity)"**. Often confused with Jurojin due to similar appearance, **Fukurokuju's extremely long head** is his distinguishing feature.

His distinguishing feature is being **accompanied by a deer**. He carries a **peach**, a symbol of longevity, and is cherished as a charm by those who wish for health and long life.

| Item | Content |
|---|---|

The **only real historical figure among the Seven Lucky Gods**. His bag is said to contain "treasures to share with people," symbolizing **"sharing and generosity,"** and he presides over good fortune in business and family life.

## What is a Seven Lucky Gods Pilgrimage? | A Small Adventure and Goshuin Collection

A **Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage** is a traditional Japanese pilgrimage where you visit **seven shrines and temples** in a region in sequence, offering prayers to each of the Seven Lucky Gods. It is an auspicious custom said to **date back to the Muromachi period**, traditionally undertaken especially during **New Year's (from New Year's Day to Matsunouchi, around January 15th)**.

### Blessings of a Seven Lucky Gods Pilgrimage

It is believed that by visiting all seven deities, one will experience "**Shichinan Sokumetsu, Shichifuku Sokushﾅ・*" (荳・屮蜊ｳ貊・・荳・ｦ丞叉逕・ 窶・meaning seven misfortunes will vanish, and seven fortunes will arise.

### Basic Steps for Pilgrimage

1.  **Decide on a course** (refer to famous routes below).
2.  **Receive a dedicated "shikishi" (colored paper board) or "Goshuincho" (Goshuin ledger)** at the first shrine or temple.
3.  **Visit each shrine or temple 竊・Offer prayers and receive a Goshuin or a Seven Lucky Gods stamp.**
4.  **Complete the pilgrimage by visiting all seven shrines/temples 竊・Display the completed shikishi/Goshuincho for one year.**
5.  **Embark on the pilgrimage again in the following New Year.**

## How to Collect Goshuin and Shikishi

### Shikishi (Colored Paper Board) Style

This style involves collecting Goshuin from seven shrines and temples on a **large shikishi (colored paper board)**, typically A4 size, specifically made for the Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage. **Once completed, it can be displayed in a tokonoma (alcove) or entryway**, offering aesthetic value as interior decor.

### Goshuincho (Goshuin Ledger) Style

This method involves dedicating a **two-page spread in a regular Goshuincho** to collect the Seven Lucky Gods' Goshuin. This is **suitable for those who want to manage them alongside Goshuin from other shrines and temples**.

### Daruma Style and Ema Style

Some regions offer unique collection methods, such as **"stamping the Seven Lucky Gods onto a Daruma doll" or "imprinting the seven deities onto an ema (votive horse tablet)."** While these offer a high degree of commemorative value, their larger size can make them challenging to carry home.

### Goshuin Etiquette

-   **Always offer prayers before** going to the Goshuin reception office.
-   **Wait quietly** while the Goshuin is being written.
-   **A Hatsuhoryo (offering fee) of ﾂ･300 is common** (some regions or establishments may charge ﾂ･500).
-   **Prepare small change** to avoid needing change.
-   **Do not treat it like a stamp rally** 窶・regard it as a sacred seal.

For detailed Goshuin etiquette, refer to the [Complete Guide to Basic Shrine Visits](/blog/shrine-visit-basics/).

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. Can I do a Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage outside of the New Year's period?**

A. **It depends on the course**. Many Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimages officially run from **New Year's Day to Matsunouchi (January 7th or 15th)**, and Goshuin are only granted during this period. However, there are also **year-round courses** (such as the Kamakura Enoshima Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage), so please check the official information for each course.

**Q2. Do I have to visit all of them in one day?**

A. **It's perfectly fine to split it over several days**. If you bring your shikishi or Goshuincho, you can continue the pilgrimage on subsequent days. However, it is traditional to **"complete it within the same pilgrimage period."** If it's during the New Year's period, splitting it over two or three days is not a problem.

**Q3. Can I visit only the deity I want to pray to the most?**

A. **There's no problem with that**. For example, if you only wish for "business prosperity," visiting just Ebisu and Daikokuten is acceptable. For "academic success," Benzaiten alone is fine. However, the complete form of a "**Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage**" involves all seven shrines and temples, so if you want to maximize your blessings, a full pilgrimage is recommended.

**Q4. What if I have children, am elderly, or don't have much physical stamina?**

A. It's recommended to start with a **compact Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage** (the Nihonbashi Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage can be completed in an hour, covering about 3 km on foot). There are also courses where you can use taxis or cars. **On rainy days or extremely hot days, it's best to postpone** rather than push yourself.

**Q5. Can I use home statues and do a Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimage concurrently?**

A. **That's actually ideal!** Displaying the shikishi received during your pilgrimage in the entryway and placing statues in the living room helps **"spiritual awareness become a daily habit."** After a year, some families keep the old shikishi (it's fine to keep displaying it) and add a new one from the current year.
