Howlite belongs to the relatively rare **borate mineral** group, with a chemical composition of **calcium and boron-containing silicate**. It's a relatively new stone, named after the **Canadian geologist Henry How** who discovered it in 1868, but its unique appearance quickly gained global popularity.

Howlite's most striking feature is its **"white base with gray to black web-like patterns (matrix)."** In Japanese aesthetics, these patterns are often likened to **"Karesansui" (dry landscape gardens)**, making it a popular choice for modern Japanese-style accessories as the **"stone that awakens the spirit of Zen."**

With a Mohs hardness of 3 to 3.5, it is **extremely soft for a gemstone**, to the point where it can be scratched with a fingernail. For daily wear, it's essential to **avoid strong impacts and friction**, and it should be understood as a stone primarily **"for admiration and as a charm."**

## <span id="meaning"></span>Stone Properties and Historical and Cultural Background

Howlite's stone properties are "**mental stability, improved sleep, patience, and calmness**."

In 1868, **Dr. Henry How** discovered and described it as a new mineral in a **gypsum deposit** in Nova Scotia, Canada. Initially, it was often kept in laboratories as a rare academic mineral, and its **appearance in the jewelry market is relatively recent, in the latter half of the 20th century**.

Since the 1970s, as part of the **"New Age movement,"** its symbolism of **"mental stability and improved sleep"** gained attention, and it rapidly became popular worldwide as a **"healing stone."** Around the same time, **howlite dyed blue** became widely available under trade names like "Howlite Turquoise" and "White Turquoise" as a **substitute for turquoise**, and it remains one of the staple products in the jewelry market today.

In Japan, from the **late 1990s to the 2000s**, its **"Japanese aesthetic reminiscent of Karesansui"** was re-evaluated, establishing it as a material for **Japanese-style accessories and power stone bracelets**. As a unique stone that deeply resonates with the Japanese sense of beauty – **"the purification of white and the tranquility of gray"** – it continues to be cherished to this day.

## <span id="care"></span>Cleansing Methods and Care

Howlite has a **Mohs hardness of 3 to 3.5**, making it a soft stone that requires **delicate cleansing methods**.

Acceptable Cleansing Methods:

-   **Placing on a Quartz Cluster or Tumbled Stones**: The safest and most common cleansing method.
-   **Passing through White Sage Smoke**: 1 to 2 minutes is sufficient.
-   **Moonlight Bath**: Place by a window 3 days before and after a full moon.
-   **Sound Bath with Tuning Fork or Crystal Tuner**: Effective if not touching metal.

Cleansing Methods to Avoid:

-   **Burying in Coarse Salt for Extended Periods** (soft stone, risk of surface damage)
-   **Ultrasonic Cleaner** (potential for cracks to expand)
-   **Prolonged Running Water** (short periods are fine, but long exposure risks surface deterioration)
-   **Prolonged Exposure to Direct Sunlight** (**dyed items like Howlite Turquoise risk fading**)

For daily care, **gently wiping with a soft cloth** is fundamental. If sweat or sebum accumulates, quickly rinse it with lukewarm water and dry it immediately. The key to preventing surface scratches is to **avoid using fingernails or bumping it against other stones due to its softness**.

## <span id="fake"></span>Distinguishing Natural from Dyed Howlite

-   **Natural howlite** has a **white to off-white base with irregular gray to black web-like patterns**. **The pattern varies completely from stone to stone**.
-   **Howlite Turquoise (dyed product)**: **Howlite dyed blue** is circulated as a turquoise substitute. It is sold under names such as "**dyed howlite," "white turquoise," and "magnesite turquoise**." It is **not genuine natural turquoise**, so be mindful of its price and handling.
-   **Howlite and Magnesite Confusion**: **Magnesite** is also a white stone with web-like patterns, and the two can be confused by trade names. Mineralogically, they are distinct.
-   **Glass Imitations**: There are also imitations made of white glass with painted web-like patterns. These can be distinguished from genuine stones by **weight, coolness to the touch, and texture**.
-   **Choosing items with a certification** is the most reliable method.

"**Howlite Turquoise = dyed howlite**" is a crucial point to remember. It's common for beginners to **mistakenly buy dyed howlite thinking it's natural turquoise**, so if the **price of turquoise is unusually low**, it's generally wise to suspect it's dyed howlite. While dyed howlite isn't a bad choice in the sense that it can be **"enjoyed affordably as a turquoise substitute,"** it's correct to acquire it with the understanding that it is **"not real turquoise."**

## <span id="faq"></span>Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. How long can Howlite be worn?**

A. With a Mohs hardness of 3 to 3.5, it is **considered soft for a gemstone**, to the point where it can be scratched with a fingernail. If **handled carefully, avoiding strong impacts and friction**, it can be enjoyed for decades, but it is not the type of stone to be **passed down through generations like diamonds or sapphires**. A realistic approach is to consider it a "**charm for several years**."

**Q2. Is it okay to wear it in the bath, pool, or during sports?**

A. Please consider **all of these activities as "not okay."** As a soft stone, hot water, chlorine, and friction are all major adversaries. Developing the **habit of "taking it off"** will ultimately make it last much longer.

**Q3. Is it true that Howlite helps with insomnia?**

A. This has **not been medically or scientifically proven**. Its effectiveness as a charm in modern times stems from a combination of its traditional symbolism as a "**stone that calms the mind**" and the **visual calming effect of the color white**. The proper way to approach it is to use it as a "**mental support for dealing with insomnia**," rather than expecting it to "cure insomnia." For severe insomnia, please prioritize seeking medical attention.

**Q4. Is Howlite Turquoise "real turquoise"?**

A. **No, it is not real turquoise**. It is a **substitute made of howlite dyed blue**, and it is sometimes called "turquoise" by trade name. **Genuine natural turquoise** typically costs several tens to hundreds of dollars per carat, and **many "turquoise bracelets" sold for a few to ten dollars per strand are dyed howlite**. If you want genuine turquoise, we recommend purchasing from a store that provides **certification**.

**Q5. Are Howlite and Magnesite the same stone?**

A. **They are different minerals**. Both have a **white base with gray to black web-like patterns** and are used as **materials for turquoise-style dyeing**, so they can be confused by trade names, but:
-   **Howlite**: Borate mineral (containing calcium and boron)
-   **Magnesite**: Carbonate mineral (containing magnesium)
They are distinct minerals with different chemical compositions.

**Q6. What should I do if my Howlite accessory chips or breaks?**

A. As a soft stone, it is prone to chipping. In the world of power stones, there's a tradition of viewing this as the stone having "**taken the damage on your behalf**." It's common to return it to the earth with gratitude or carefully store it in a drawer. Since it is in an **affordable price range**, it's also a good point of Howlite that it's easy to welcome a new stone if one chips.
