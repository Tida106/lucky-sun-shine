## <span id="basic"></span>Basic Information and Mineral Data

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Stone Name | Tourmaline |
| Japanese Name | Denki-seki (Electric Stone) |
| Mineral Species | Tourmaline Group (Complex Silicate) |
| Crystal System | Trigonal |
| Main Sources | Brazil, Sri Lanka, Madagascar, Myanmar, Afghanistan, USA |
| Mohs Hardness | 7 to 7.5 |
| Specific Gravity | 3.06 to 3.25 |
| Refractive Index | 1.624 to 1.644 |
| Colors | Almost all colors (colorless, pink, red, green, blue, black, bi-color, etc.) |

Tourmaline is a **group name encompassing over 11 mineral species with differing chemical compositions**. While sharing a common crystal structure, **trace elements cause variations in color**. For example, high iron content leads to black (Schorl), lithium to diverse colors (Elbaite), and magnesium to brown (Dravite) — the color is determined by the locality and composition.

The most prominent features of Tourmaline are **"pyroelectricity"** and **"piezoelectricity"** — physical phenomena where it **becomes electrically charged when heated or subjected to pressure/friction**. Historically known as **"the stone that gathers ash,"** the observation that heated tourmaline attracted tobacco ash led to its Japanese name, "**Denki-seki (Electric Stone)**."

With a Mohs hardness of 7 to 7.5, it is sufficiently durable for daily use. However, it is **vulnerable to sudden temperature changes**, so caution is advised when handling it in baths or kitchens.

## <span id="meaning"></span>Stone Lore and Historical and Cultural Background

The general meanings associated with Tourmaline are "**Patience, Hope, Generosity, and Friendship**." Its etymology comes from the Sinhala word (Sri Lanka) "**turamali = stone of mixed colors**," which became established when Dutch merchants brought it back from Sri Lanka in the 18th century.

In ancient Egypt, a myth tells of Tourmaline being **"a stone that traveled from the center of the Earth across a rainbow to reach the surface,"** and its diverse colors were believed to be **"fragments of the rainbow."** In 1703, Dutch merchants noticed the strange electrification of heated Tourmaline, which they called "**the stone that gathers ash**," sparking significant interest in the European scientific community. This led to its Japanese name "Electric Stone" and became the origin of the modern legend of **"a stone that generates negative ions."**

**Empress Dowager Cixi** of China's Qing Dynasty was known to have extensively collected pink Tourmaline (especially from California), considering it "**the stone she loved most among all her jewelry**." It is said that tens of kilograms of pink Tourmaline were buried with her in her tomb.

In the late 20th century (1989), **"neon blue glowing Tourmaline"** was discovered in Paraíba, Brazil, and **Paraíba Tourmaline** quickly swept the global gem market. Today, it stands as one of the most prized gemstones, with prices reaching several million yen per carat.

> Source: GIA (Gemological Institute of America) Tourmaline Overview, AGTA (American Gem Trade Association) Gemological Material, etc.

## <span id="care"></span>Purification Methods and Care

Tourmaline has a **Mohs hardness of 7 to 7.5**, making it quite durable and **compatible with most purification methods**.

-   **Place on a Quartz cluster or small chips**: A classic overnight purification method.
-   **Pass through White Sage smoke**: 1 to 2 minutes is sufficient.
-   **Moonlight bathing**: Place by a window for three days before and after the full moon.
-   **Running water**: Briefly rinse with lukewarm water if desired.
-   **Sound bath with a tuning fork or crystal tuner**: Effective if careful not to touch metal.

Purification methods to avoid:

-   **Burying in coarse salt for extended periods** (may damage the surface).
-   **Prolonged use of an ultrasonic cleaner** (may enlarge cracks in varieties with many inclusions).
-   **Sudden temperature changes** (its pyroelectric nature can cause thermal shock and cracking).
-   **Prolonged exposure to direct sunlight** (some color varieties, especially pink tourmaline, may fade).

For daily care, **gently wipe with a soft cloth** is fundamental. If sweat or sebum is a concern, quickly rinse with lukewarm water and dry immediately.

## <span id="fake"></span>Distinguishing Natural, Synthetic, and Treated Stones

-   **Natural Tourmaline** typically exhibits **pleochroism (color changes depending on the viewing angle)**. **Inclusions (thread-like internal features)** are also natural.
-   **Synthetic Tourmaline** is very rare in circulation, and the jewelry market primarily deals with **natural specimens**.
-   **Heat treatment** is a standard process performed on some pink Tourmaline and Paraíba Tourmaline.
-   **Irradiation treatment** enhances color. As it can reduce value, inquire when purchasing.
-   **Similar stones**: Synthetic glass (watch out for multi-colored ones), dyed quartz, etc.
-   **Choosing one with a gemological certificate (e.g., GIA, CGL) is the most reliable method.**

Especially for **Paraíba Tourmaline**, which is in the high price range (hundreds of thousands of yen per carat), the value varies depending on whether it is from **Brazil, Mozambique, or Nigeria**. **Verification of origin with a gemological certificate** is essential.

## <span id="faq"></span>Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. How long can Tourmaline be worn?**

A. With a Mohs hardness of 7 to 7.5, it is sufficiently durable for daily use and has the longevity to be **passed down through generations**. While the platinum and gold settings may wear down in 10 to 20 years, with regular **re-setting or re-polishing of the metalwork**, the stone itself can be enjoyed almost indefinitely.

**Q2. Is it okay to wear it while bathing, swimming, or playing sports?**

A. **Short baths are generally okay**, but **sudden temperature changes (hot water to cold water)** should be avoided due to its pyroelectric properties. Chlorine in pools and sulfur in hot springs can discolor the metal settings, and sports can cause impacts that may enlarge cracks. It is best to **develop a habit of removing it** to ensure its longevity.

**Q3. Is the "negative ion effect" of Tourmaline real?**

A. **It has not been scientifically proven**. This claim was widely promoted in health product advertisements after the 1990s, but no proven health benefits from wearing it have been confirmed. The name "**Electric Stone**" for Tourmaline derives from its **physical property of becoming electrically charged through heating and friction**, which is separate from any human health effects. Enjoying its **symbolic significance as an amulet** is the intended way to interact with it.

**Q4. What is the difference between Black Tourmaline and Morion?**

A. **Although similar in appearance, they are different minerals**. **Black Tourmaline (Schorl)** is a high-iron species within the Tourmaline group, while **Morion (Black Quartz)** is a black variety of Quartz. Both are "**classic protective stones**," but Black Tourmaline possesses **properties as an electric stone**, while Morion has **versatility as Quartz**.

**Q5. Can I wear Tourmaline even if I'm not born in October?**

A. Absolutely! Birthstones are merely **"stones with particularly good compatibility,"** and there is no problem for those born in other months who wish to resonate with Tourmaline's lore of "Patience and Hope." Due to its **diversity of color varieties**, Tourmaline is cherished by a wide range of wearers, regardless of their birth month, as it allows them to **"choose according to their current purpose."**

**Q6. What is Paraíba Tourmaline?**

A. **A rare color variety discovered in Paraíba, Brazil, in 1989**, it glows with a **neon blue to turquoise blue** color due to the **presence of copper and manganese**. While subsequent discoveries were made in Mozambique and Nigeria, **Brazilian Paraíba remains the most prized**. It is an **investment-grade gemstone**, costing several million yen per carat, and holds a unique status within the entire Tourmaline family.
