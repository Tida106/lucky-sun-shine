## Introduction: Easing Worries About "Is it Okay to Dispose of Them? Will I Be Punished?"

Do you have old omamori (amulets) tucked away in a drawer, lucky charms you bought with hopes but no longer use, or broken power stones?

Many people find themselves unable to part with these items for a long time, worried that "I might be punished if I throw them away" or "I don't know the right way to dispose of them, so I've just left them."

First, let me tell you something important.

> **It's not a bad thing to let go with gratitude.** Lucky charms and omamori are items that "have fulfilled their role." Because you cherished them, arranging a proper farewell and letting them go is a sign of respect, and it's also preparation for welcoming new blessings.

Instead of thinking of it as "disposal," try to read through with the image of "**returning with gratitude**" or "**allowing them to complete their role**."

> **Please Note**: The practices described in this article are based on Japanese traditional culture and customs. They do not guarantee scientific effects, nor does failing to follow these steps absolutely lead to misfortune. It's perfectly fine to approach this in a way that feels comfortable and right for you, without undue stress.

## When to Let Go: Timing for Disposal

### Guidelines by Type

| Lucky Charm | Recommended Time to Let Go |
|---|---|
| Omamori (amulets) and Ofuda (talismans) | **One year** (typically returned around the New Year) |
| Kumade (lucky rake) and Hamaya (demon-breaking arrow) | **One year** (exchanged at the next Tori-no-ichi market or New Year's visit) |
| Daruma doll | **One year** or **when your wish has come true** |
| Maneki-neko (beckoning cat) and other figurines | **When broken or damaged** (no specific time limit) |
| Power Stones | **When purification no longer restores them, when broken, or when your gratitude fades** |

### Other Signs It's Time to Let Go

- Your wish has already been granted.
- Your feelings have changed, and you no longer feel a connection to that lucky charm.
- Your life stage has shifted, such as moving or changing jobs.
- You've been looking at it for a long time and no longer feel "thank you."

Rather than keeping items for many years, thinking "maybe I can still use this," it's believed that **letting go while you still feel gratitude** allows the lucky charm to fully complete its original purpose.

## Disposal Methods by Type

### ① Omamori and Ofuda

Omamori and Ofuda received from shrines or temples have been treated as **extensions of gods and Buddhas**. Instead of simply putting them in general trash, the traditional Japanese custom is to **express gratitude and then return them**.

**Main Return Methods:**

- **Bringing them to a Kofuda Nōsho (Old Talisman Return Point)**: Many shrines and temples have these on their grounds, often accepting items year-round.
- **Dondo-yaki (Sagicho)**: A fire festival held around January 15th at local shrines or public spaces, where New Year decorations are ritually burned.
- **Mail-in Returns and Otakiage Services**: Convenient for omamori from distant shrines or if there isn't a shrine nearby.

> **"Can I return an omamori received from one shrine to a different one?"** → In Shinto, this is generally considered acceptable. Feel free to inquire at a local shrine. However, confirming in advance is always recommended for peace of mind.

For more detailed procedures on disposing of omamori and ofuda, including handling items from distant shrines and at-home memorial practices, please refer to [Complete Guide to Disposing of Lucky Charms](/blog/engimono-disposal/).

### ② Daruma Dolls

**Ritual burning (Otakiage)** is the primary method for Daruma dolls.

-   **Dondo-yaki**: Bring them to a local fire festival (limited to combustible materials).
-   **Daruma Kuyo (Memorial Service for Daruma)**: Some temples, like Shorinzan Daruma-ji in Takasaki, Gunma, specialize in performing Daruma memorial services.
-   **Return to the Shrine or Temple where purchased**: This is the smoothest option if accepted.

If your wish has been granted, the custom is to **draw in both eyes** before returning the Daruma.

### ③ Kumade and Hamaya

**Returning them to the shrine or temple where they were purchased (or received)** is the most straightforward method.

-   **Kumade**: At the Tori-no-ichi market the following year, there is often a "return old kumade" corner. Exchanging them there is traditional.
-   **Hamaya**: Bring to the Kofuda Nōsho during your New Year's visit the following year. Many shrines accept them even if they weren't the original giver.

If it's difficult to visit a shrine, using a mail-in ritual burning service is another option.

### ④ Maneki-neko, Figurines, and Other Lucky Decorations

These items **do not have a strict expiration date**. The time to let go is when they are broken or damaged, or when your feelings change.

**Disposal Procedure (for those who prefer a respectful approach):**

1.  Wrap the item in white paper (rice paper or even copy paper is fine).
2.  Sprinkle a pinch of salt over it for purification.
3.  Place your hands together and express gratitude, saying "Thank you very much."
4.  If combustible, place in combustible waste; if ceramic or glass, place in non-combustible waste (in a separate bag from other trash).

You could also inquire at a shrine's Kofuda Nōsho to see if they can accept them (depending on material and size, they might).

### ⑤ Power Stones

Power stones have a different origin than shrine omamori, so their disposal methods are considered more flexible.

**First things to try: Purification**

When you feel it might be time to let go, first try purifying your power stone following the [Complete Guide to Power Stone Purification Methods](/blog/purification-complete-guide/). It's believed that purification can sometimes restore your connection with the stone.

**If you still decide to let go:**

-   **Return to the Earth**: Bury it shallowly in garden soil or potting soil. This is an environmentally friendly method, believed to purify and return the stone to the earth's energy.
-   **Consult a Shrine or Temple**: Some may accept them if you inquire in advance.
-   **Dispose with Gratitude**: Wrap it in white paper, sprinkle salt, express your thanks, and then place it in non-combustible waste (as stones do not burn).

## What Not to Do

While there's no "absolute correct" way to dispose of items, from the perspective of respecting lucky charms, here are some **things to avoid**.

| NG Action | Reason |
|---|---|
| Carelessly throwing it in with other trash without thought | You'll be letting go without the opportunity to express gratitude. |
| Personally burning it in your garden or on a stove at home | Could violate fire safety regulations and cause neighborly disputes. |
| Discarding into rivers, seas, or ponds | Leads to environmental pollution and is considered illegal dumping. |
| Selling on flea market apps or at second-hand shops | Omamori and similar items are given for personal protection and blessings. Reselling is culturally considered inappropriate. |
| Giving to others | It's considered contrary to custom to transfer an omamori received for oneself to another person. |

> You might hear stories that "carelessly discarding them will bring punishment," but what's more important is the **"feeling" involved**. Expressing even a single word of thanks to something that has been with you for a long time before letting it go — I believe that's perfectly sufficient.

## After Disposal: The Concept of "Circulation" in Welcoming New Items

At the root of the lucky charm culture is the idea that **"by letting go, new blessings and connections can enter your life."**

You return old omamori, so you can receive new ones with fresh wishes.
You let go of power stones that have fulfilled their role with gratitude, so you can encounter the next stone that calls to you.
You return the kumade you used for a year, so you can rake in even greater fortune with a new, larger kumade.

**"Disposal" isn't an end, but the beginning of the next cycle.**

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q: Is it a problem if I've kept an expired omamori for a long time?**

A: No, there's no problem. The "one-year guideline" is simply a way to help you refresh your feelings and make a clean break for the new year. Keeping it for many years won't bring you misfortune. If you're concerned, returning it to a shrine during your next New Year's visit will help you feel refreshed.

**Q: Can I return an omamori from a distant shrine to a nearby shrine?**

A: Yes, generally it will be accepted. In Shinto, there's a belief that "gods do not contend with each other," so it's usually fine to return an omamori to a different shrine than where you received it. Checking in advance is always reassuring.

**Q: Will I really be punished if I throw an omamori in regular trash?**

A: Culturally, it's considered "disrespectful," but scientifically, there's no punishment. What's most important is "whether you are letting go with a feeling of gratitude." If you absolutely cannot go to a shrine, disposing of it by wrapping it in white paper and expressing your thanks (at-home memorial) is perfectly sufficient.

**Q: Is a broken power stone an ominous sign?**

A: It is not considered an ominous sign. Stones often break due to physical impact, deterioration, or temperature changes. Please refer to [Meaning and What to Do When a Power Stone Breaks or Bracelet Snaps](/blog/powerstone-broken-meaning/) for more details.

**Q: Can I return multiple omamori together?**

A: Yes, that's fine. You can gather them in white paper and place them in the Kofuda Nōsho. Remember to express your gratitude in your heart, saying "Thank you for the past year," before placing them.

**Q: Is there a fee for disposing of lucky charms?**

A: Returning items to a shrine's Kofuda Nōsho is generally free (some people optionally offer a small donation). If you use a mail-in ritual burning service, the cost is typically around 3,000 to 10,000 yen.

## Summary

-   Letting go with gratitude is not a bad thing; it's an **act of allowing lucky charms to fulfill their role**.
-   There are no "absolute must-follow" rules for disposal. **Putting your heart into it is what truly matters**.
-   Omamori and Ofuda can be returned via **Kofuda Nōsho, Dondo-yaki, or Otakiage services**.
-   For Daruma dolls, **draw in both eyes before ritual burning**. For Kumade and Hamaya, **return them to the shrine where you received them** is the basic practice.
-   Maneki-neko and figurines should be **purified with salt and thanked before disposal** (ceramic items go into non-combustible waste).
-   For power stones, **try purification first**. If you still let go, return them to the earth or consult a shrine.
-   **Once you let go, you become ready to welcome new things.** View it positively as a cycle.
