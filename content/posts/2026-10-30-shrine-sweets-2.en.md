## Introduction

In [Part 1](/blog/shrine-sweets/) we paired five shrines with the sweets to eat after praying. Part 2 adds **eight more temple-gate treats from across Japan**, from the pigeon-shaped biscuits of Kamakura and the ningyo-yaki of Asakusa to a tengu-faced cake on Mount Takao and the blue-and-red striped mochi of Taga. Each one is tied to a deity, a legend, or a wish.

Many of these are easy to bring home, so they also make good souvenirs for visitors from abroad.

## <span id="hato"></span>1. Hato Sablé (Tsurugaoka Hachimangu, Kamakura)

Tsurugaoka Hachimangu is Kamakura's best-known shrine, and many visitors leave with a box of pigeon-shaped biscuits.

**The story:** Toshimaya's founder is said to have been so impressed by a Western biscuit given to him by a foreigner that he spent years trying to recreate it. He was devoted to Hachiman, the deity of the shrine. The character for "eight" (八) on the plaque of the main hall is a design made by combining pigeons, and pigeons on the grounds were beloved by children. That is said to be how the "pigeon sablé" got its name and shape.

**How it tastes:** A crisp, buttery biscuit. The cute pigeon shape makes it a pleasure to look at even after you get home.

**A note:** Hachiman is widely regarded as a deity of victory, so it is a fitting treat to bring back before an important exam or match.

## <span id="ningyo"></span>2. Ningyo-yaki (Senso-ji, Tokyo)

Senso-ji in Asakusa enshrines Kannon, the bodhisattva of compassion. Shops on and around the grounds sell freshly baked ningyo-yaki, a staple souvenir for visitors.

**The story:** Ningyo-yaki is said to have originated in Ningyocho in Tokyo's Nihonbashi district. As the name ("doll cake") suggests, it is baked in the shapes of faces, bodies and animals. The long-established Asakusa shop Kimuraya Ningyoyaki Honpo is described as dating back to the early Meiji era.

**How it tastes:** A simple cake with sweet bean filling, baked in a mold. At Kimuraya the molds are a pigeon, a lantern, the thunder god and a five-story pagoda, and watching them baked at the shopfront is part of the fun. The photo shows ningyo-yaki from the long-established Tokiwado.

**A note:** With so many cute shapes, it is a perfect keepsake of your visit.

## <span id="tengu"></span>3. Tengu-yaki (Takao-san Yakuo-in, Tokyo)

Mount Takao is a sacred mountain near Tokyo. At Yakuo-in, the attendants of the deity Iizuna Gongen are said to be **tengu**, long-nosed or crow-faced mountain spirits, and a crow tengu statue stands in front of the temple's main shrine.

**What is it?** A grilled cake pressed in a tengu-face mold, a bit like taiyaki. The crisp outside and chewy inside hold sweet **black soybean paste** made with Hokkaido beans. It is reported to be sold basically only at **Takaosan Sumika**, next to Takaosan Station on the cable car.

## <span id="mitarashi"></span>4. Mitarashi Dango (Shimogamo Shrine, Kyoto)

In front of the World Heritage-listed Shimogamo Shrine stands **Kamo Mitarashi Tea House**, regarded as the birthplace of mitarashi dango.

**The story:** In the Kamakura period, at the shrine's **Mitarashi Festival**, dumplings shaped like the bubbles rising in the sacred Mitarashi pond are said to have been offered to the deity. The tea house itself was founded in 1922.

**How it tastes:** Grilled dumplings coated in a sweet soy glaze. Each skewer holds five, with the first set slightly apart from the rest. The five are said to represent **the five parts of the human body, with the meaning of warding off misfortune**.

**Best way to enjoy it:** Sit down at the tea house after your visit with a cup of tea. The sweet-salty glaze tastes especially good after a walk through the green grounds.

## <span id="kiyome"></span>5. Kiyome Mochi (Atsuta Jingu, Nagoya)

Atsuta Jingu enshrines the sacred sword Kusanagi, one of Japan's Three Sacred Treasures. Its classic Nagoya souvenir is **kiyome mochi**.

**The story:** Around 1785, in the middle of the Edo period, a **"Kiyome Chaya" (purification tea house)** was set up at Atsuta Jingu. Visitors rested over tea there and straightened themselves before praying at the shrine. **The mochi was named after that tea house.**

**How it tastes:** Smooth red bean paste wrapped in soft habutae mochi, stamped with the word **"kiyome"**. Elegantly sweet, with a chestnut version too.

Taga Taisha, lovingly called **"Otaga-san"** by locals, is revered for long life, matchmaking and warding off misfortune. Confectioners around the shrine sell its signature itokiri mochi.

## <span id="kibi"></span>7. Kibi Dango (Kibitsu Shrine, Okayama)

Kibitsu Shrine in Okayama is said to be the setting of **the tale of subduing the ogre Ura, the prototype of the Momotaro legend**. Its main deity, Okibitsuhiko no Mikoto, is said to have defeated the Ura clan, and that story is considered the root of Momotaro.

<figure class="photo">
<img src="/images/posts/kibitsu-jinja-1200.webp" srcset="/images/posts/kibitsu-jinja-640.webp 640w, /images/posts/kibitsu-jinja-1200.webp 1200w" sizes="(min-width: 768px) 720px, 100vw" width="1200" height="476" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="The side-by-side gabled roofs of Kibitsu Shrine">

</figure>

**Highlights:** The main hall and worship hall are National Treasures. Their unusual double-gabled shape is called **"hiyoku irimoya-zukuri" (Kibitsu-zukuri)** and is said to exist nowhere else in Japan. A pine-lined approach about 450 meters long leads from the station.

**The story:** Kibi dango began as dumplings made from millet flour. By the Edo and Meiji periods, a soft gyuhi made from glutinous rice, flavored with millet flour, had become almost the same recipe as today's, and its long shelf life made it popular as a travel companion and souvenir.

**How it tastes:** Soft and gently sweet. You can also find Okayama-style versions made with white peach or muscat grapes.

## <span id="kyuman"></span>8. Kyu-man (Kotohira-gu, Kagawa)

Kotohira-gu, lovingly called "Konpira-san," is famous for its long flight of stone steps. On the approach stands **Kyu-man Honpo Ishidan-ya**.

**The story:** The shop began as an inn for pilgrims to Konpira. As the story goes, a maid placed gentle moxa on a handsome guest and it became known as **"a sweet moxa that really works."** When the inn became a confectioner, it named its manju, shaped like a small mound of moxa, **"kyu-man."**

**How it tastes:** A lightly sweet manju made with fine egg-yolk bean paste. It has a reputation for **easing the fatigue of the climb**, and you can rest inside the shop.

**A note:** It is 785 steps to the main shrine. Eat one before you climb or as a reward afterward.

## <span id="tips"></span>Tips for Pairing Prayer and Sweets

- **Pray first, eat after.** Finish your visit, then enjoy the sweet as a quiet thank-you.
- **Eat outside the grounds.** Avoid eating inside; sit at a shop or rest area on the approach.
- **Check shelf life for souvenirs.** Many fresh sweets keep only a few days, so look at the date when you buy.
- **Check where it is sold.** Some, like tengu-yaki, are sold in only one place.
- **Avoid peak hours.** Weekend lunchtime lines are long; mornings are easier.

## <span id="faq"></span>Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1. Can I eat on the grounds?**

A. Rules vary, but it is safest to **avoid eating on the grounds** and to enjoy sweets at shops or rest areas along the approach.

**Q2. Can I visit all eight places on one trip?**

A. They are spread across Kanagawa, Tokyo, Kyoto, Aichi, Shiga, Okayama and Kagawa. In the Kanto region you can combine **Kamakura, Asakusa and Mount Takao**, but we suggest **one or two places per trip** overall.

**Q3. Where can I buy tengu-yaki?**

A. It is reported to be sold at Takaosan Sumika, next to Takaosan Station on the cable car. Check opening hours and availability before you go.

**Q4. Which ones make good souvenirs?**

A. Hato sablé and kibi dango are known for keeping well, but it varies by product and shop, so **please check the date when you buy**.
