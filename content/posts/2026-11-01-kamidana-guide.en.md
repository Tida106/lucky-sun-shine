Have you ever wondered how to enshrine a kamidana properly? A kamidana is a small shelf-shrine that welcomes the kami into a Japanese home. This guide covers its history, where to put it, how to arrange the talismans, and the basic offerings.

## What is a kamidana?

In Shinto, the kami are not thought of as always being in one place; they are welcomed when they are enshrined. For that reason, it is believed that ancient Japan had no kamidana.

## History

- Around the Kamakura to Muromachi periods, priests of the Ise Shrines (called *oshi*) are said to have traveled the country distributing shrine talismans.
- In the Edo period, pilgrimages to Ise became a huge boom. The oshi devised the "Daijingu-dana," and the custom of enshrining Ise talismans at home spread and took root among ordinary people.

So the household kamidana is said to have spread along with devotion to Ise from the mid-Edo period. See our [Ise Jingu guide](/en/blog/ise-jingu-power-spot/) too.

## Where and which way to place it

Generally, the following are considered good:

- **Direction**: facing south or east
- **Height**: above eye level
- **Environment**: bright, clean, with sunlight and airflow, and easy to worship at every day
- **Places to avoid**: back-to-back with a toilet, above a busy doorway, or facing a Buddhist altar in the same room
- **In a two-story house**: the upper floor is preferred
- **On top of a chest of drawers**: lay down white cloth or white paper first

When enshrining one for the first time, it is said to be good to ask your local shrine (ujigami) to perform a purification ceremony.

## How to enshrine the talismans (ofuda)

A kamidana usually holds three kinds of ofuda:

- **Jingu Taima**: the talisman of Ise Jingu (Kotai Jingu), distributed through local shrines. It is customary to renew it at the start of the year.
- **Ujigami ofuda**: the talisman of your local shrine.
- **Favorite-shrine ofuda**: from a shrine you personally revere.

The arrangement depends on the shrine's doors:

- **Three doors**: Jingu Taima in the center, ujigami on the right, favorite shrine on the left (as you face it)
- **One door**: stack them front to back in this order: Jingu Taima, ujigami, favorite shrine

Customs can differ by shrine, so if you are unsure, ask your local shrine.

## Basic offerings

- **Basics**: rice, sake, salt, and water
- **Replacement**: rice, salt and water every morning; sakaki branches twice a month (around the 1st and 15th)
- **One example of layout**: water, rice, salt from left to right
- **Often avoided**: meat, dairy, and onion-family vegetables (this varies by region and deity)
- **Otsagari**: after the offering, the food is received with gratitude

For sake, see [Why sake and shrines are so closely linked](/en/blog/sake-and-shrines/).

## Daily worship

As at a shrine, **two bows, two claps, one bow** is the general form. After changing the offerings in the morning, place your hands together in gratitude. For shrine etiquette, see our [shrine visit basics](/en/blog/shrine-visit-basics/).

## If you have no space for one

There are compact, modern wall-mounted kamidana. Some people also lay white paper on a high shelf and stand the ofuda on it. For where to place omamori, see our [omamori guide](/en/blog/omamori-guide/).

## Year-end cleaning and returning old ofuda

At year's end, it is common to clean the kamidana and welcome new ofuda. Old ofuda are returned to a shrine's collection point for old talismans.
