## Introduction

Have you ever wondered, "Why are there crossed beams on the roof?" or "Why does this shrine look different from the last one?" The honden (main sanctuary) of a shrine comes in **many architectural styles, depending on the enshrined deity, the region and the history**.

This article starts with the easiest clues, **the direction of the entrance and the shape of the roof**, then introduces chigi and katsuogi, materials and construction, and eight major honden styles. Knowing them will make looking up at the honden on your next visit much more fun.

## <span id="basic"></span>Shrine Architecture Basics: Honden, Heiden and Haiden

At the center of a shrine is the **honden (main sanctuary)**, where the deity is enshrined. At many shrines, the **heiden (offering hall)** stands in front of it, and the **haiden (worship hall)**, where visitors pray, stands in front of that. What we usually pray before is the haiden; the honden stands quietly behind it.

Whereas a Buddhist temple often has a large hall that people enter to worship, a shrine honden is **relatively small and its interior is rarely used**. As a result, the exterior design and floor plan are richly varied, which is a major feature of shrine architecture. The forms of the honden are distinguished by the word "zukuri" (造, "style" or "construction").

## <span id="chigi"></span>Reading the Roof Ornaments: Chigi and Katsuogi

On the roof of a honden you often see **chigi** and **katsuogi**. Chigi are timbers that cross at the ends of the roof and stretch toward the sky, and katsuogi are round logs, shaped like dried bonito, that line the ridge. Having both is said to be a feature of older styles, and they are symbolic ornaments of shrine architecture.

There are ways to read them:

- **Chigi tips cut horizontally (uchi-sogi)** → a rough guide to a shrine for a goddess
- **Chigi tips cut vertically (soto-sogi)** → a rough guide to a shrine for a god
- **An even number of katsuogi** → goddess, **an odd number** → god (a rough guide)

However, this is only **a rule of thumb, and there are exceptions**. Compare them at the shrines you visit, keeping in mind that not everything follows the rule.

## <span id="material"></span>Materials and Construction: Cypress, Hiwadabuki and Rebuilding

### Wood and Roofing

Shrine buildings are basically **wooden structures made of cypress (hinoki) and similar timber**. Roofs include **hiwadabuki**, which layers cypress bark, as well as copper plate, thatch and kokerabuki (thin wooden shingles). Because cypress bark can be harvested again as the tree regrows, it is also said to embody the wisdom of protecting buildings together with the forest. The craftsmanship that supports traditional architecture, such as harvesting cypress bark and re-roofing, was inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2020.

### How the Pillars Stand

In some old styles, pillars are not set on foundation stones but **stand directly in holes dug in the ground (hottate-bashira)**. The munamochi-bashira, which support the ridge and stand outside the walls of a raised-floor building, are said to resemble ancient storehouses. The main sanctuary of Ise Jingu is the classic example.

### Rebuilding to Hand Down: Shikinen Sengu

Some shrines **rebuild their buildings at set intervals and move the deity into the new ones, a practice called Shikinen Sengu (Shikinen Zotai)**. It is best known at Ise Jingu, where it takes place every 20 years. Kamigamo Shrine and Shimogamo Shrine are also recorded as having rebuilt their halls every twenty-odd years in the past, before the practice was revived in the Edo period. Through rebuilding, **not only the form of the buildings but also the skills to make them are passed on to the next generation**.

## <span id="styles"></span>Eight Major Honden Styles

### 1. Shinmei-zukuri

**Modeled on the main sanctuary of Ise Jingu**, this is one of the oldest styles. The roof is a gabled kirizuma-zukuri roof, like an upturned book, and the entrance is on the long side of the roof (hirairi). It basically has chigi and katsuogi, munamochi-bashira and hottate-bashira, a raised floor, and a veranda with a railing all around. There is little decoration, and its charm lies in the simple beauty of unpainted wood (shiraki).

### 2. Taisha-zukuri

**Modeled on the main sanctuary of Izumo Taisha.** The entrance is on the short side of the roof (the gable end), called "tsumairi," a form said to derive from ancient dwellings. It is often seen in Shimane and Tottori Prefectures. Said to be as old as Shinmei-zukuri, it has chigi that tower above the roof.

### 3. Sumiyoshi-zukuri

**Modeled on the main sanctuary of Sumiyoshi Taisha.** Like Taisha-zukuri, the entrance is on the gable end, but **the pillars are painted vermilion and the walls white**, and the interior is divided into two rooms, front and back (outer and inner chambers). It is an ancient form that is also said to resemble buildings used in ceremonies related to the enthronement of ancient emperors.

### 4. Nagare-zukuri

**Modeled on the main sanctuaries of Kamigamo Shrine and Shimogamo Shrine.** The front side of a gabled, hirairi roof extends long in a flowing line, forming a canopy (hisashi) over the front. Halls of this style are **the most numerous in Japan, said to make up about 60 percent of shrines**. The honden and gonden of Kamigamo Shrine are designated National Treasures as classic examples of Nagare-zukuri.

### 5. Kasuga-zukuri

**Modeled on the main sanctuary of Kasuga Taisha.** It has a gabled, tsumairi roof with a canopy over the entrance, and stands on a base. Many halls are relatively small, and the style spread across Japan.

The halls are small, with raised floors and a canopy over the front of the roof. The vermilion pillars and the chigi curving up to the sky are the easy-to-spot features of Kasuga-zukuri.

## <span id="how"></span>Three Points for Telling Styles Apart

Even without memorizing the style names, checking these three points in order will tell you the rough family.

1. **Which side is the entrance on?** If the entrance is on the side where you see the roof's triangle (the gable end), it belongs to the Taisha-zukuri, Sumiyoshi-zukuri or Kasuga-zukuri group. If it is on the long side (hirairi), it belongs to the Shinmei-zukuri, Nagare-zukuri or Hachiman-zukuri group.
2. **Does a long canopy extend over the front?** If the roof flows forward, it is Nagare-zukuri.
3. **What about color and decoration?** Plain unpainted wood suggests Shinmei-zukuri, vermilion and white suggests Sumiyoshi-zukuri, and lavish carving and coloring often suggests Gongen-zukuri.

## <span id="faq"></span>FAQ

### Q. How many shrine architecture styles are there?

Shinmei-zukuri, Taisha-zukuri, Sumiyoshi-zukuri, Nagare-zukuri, Kasuga-zukuri and Hachiman-zukuri are generally known as the "major styles," and there are many others such as Gongen-zukuri, Sengen-zukuri and Hie-zukuri. Classifications differ among researchers, so there is no fixed number.

### Q. How do shrine buildings differ from temple buildings?

Temples are often built around halls that people enter to worship, whereas a shrine honden is where the deity dwells; its interior is rarely used, and many are relatively small. Chigi, katsuogi and torii are also elements unique to shrines.

### Q. Can I see the honden?

It depends on the shrine. It stands behind the haiden and is usually surrounded by fences, so you often cannot approach it. In many cases, you can see the honden roof and chigi and katsuogi over the roof of the haiden.

## <span id="summary"></span>Summary

- Shrine honden come in **many architectural styles**, such as Shinmei-zukuri, Taisha-zukuri and Nagare-zukuri
- The tips for telling them apart are **whether the entrance is on the gable end or the long side**, **whether the front canopy extends**, and **color and decoration**
- **Chigi, katsuogi, materials and construction methods** also hold the wisdom of ancient faith and skill
- When you visit, also pay attention to **the shape of the roof** beyond the haiden

On your next visit, try looking up at the honden roof. You will notice that even among "shrines," the appearance differs from place to place.
